## Current state described by the documents

The adopted MVP encrypts one canonical, seekable payload per deployment using a shared content key (SCK). A threshold provisioning system checks the requesting identity's current entitlement against settled chain state and returns the SCK in a response wrapped to that identity for an authorization window. Distinct response bytes protect delivery; the unwrapped payload key is the same for every authorized holder of that deployment.

The reference client keeps the response and decrypt-capable material in memory and destroys them, including derivatives and active cipher contexts, at expiry. Subsequent authorization requires new provisioning. The stated expiry guarantee excludes modified clients deliberately retaining material. The threshold provisioner remains a dependency on the read path.

The documents separately describe a later cooperative plaintext-fingerprinting overlay using a variant seed committed by the entitlement. That seed is not currently a construction for distinct native decryption keys. Its secret authorship is unresolved. The current proposal to allow compact decryption helpers must not be confused with this already-described fingerprint overlay.

The two original divergences remain distinct:

1. Individually wrapped responses contain a common SCK instead of native, distinct window material.
2. A threshold provisioning network resolves access instead of the desired entitlement-state and wallet-control mechanism without that network.

Relevant source sections: cryptography's **MVP Posture: Per-Window Provisioning**, **Provisioning Liveness, Centralization, and Participation**, **Recipient-Bound Decryption Credentials**, and the MVP scope's deferred **Recipient- and Window-Bound Decryption Credentials** and **Consensus-Native Provisioning**.

### Unresolved interpretations that must remain visible

1. **Window granularity.** The user proposed the ownership interval itself as a sufficient window: while the NFT remains registered to the holder. The current document also specifies bounded subwindows within an ownership interval, with settlement and volume limits. Both are research possibilities; no final replacement policy has been adopted. Every-attempt state checking remains required in either case. A single ownership interval does not require a fresh key at every check.
2. **Native capability isolation.** The document additionally prohibits exposing a common decryption-equivalent secret or deriving another window's capability. The user explicitly relaxed tracing and adversarial expiry, not the original common-SCK objection. Do not quietly treat a persistent asset master key, an easily recoverable common key, or a rerandomized wrap as satisfying R04. If a candidate needs weaker isolation, describe the exact relaxation separately. 
  *2026-09-16 addition (our deduction, S13).* Every entitled holder of a fixed common ciphertext necessarily holds a compact decryption-equivalent object — its own window material plus any helper — so R04 can only constrain what is **common**. Three readings fit the wording and select different critical paths. 
  **(A) Attributability:** the only compact objects that decrypt are per-holder and identify their holder, so leaked material is attributable in principle; only the multi-key-mask body class (C05/C07/C08) reaches this, it needs an issuer or a delegation chain, and it makes issued-key attributability (not arbitrary-decoder tracing) part of the primary requirement. 
  **(B) Issued distinctness:** the material a holder is issued or generates is per-holder even though every decrypter can derive a compact common object such as a DEM key or a per-piece key file; any per-holder-witness construction qualifies, including witness encryption applied per piece, and the remaining divergence is the provisioner (Q02). 
  **(C) Custody:** no party other than an entitled holder ever holds a compact common object at rest; this excludes a provisioner holding an SCK and an issuer holding a traceable-PRF master key, admits a witness-only path whose per-piece keys exist only as a holder's derived values, and routes to Q02. *User decision, 2026-09-16 (log 14):* **(C) is excluded** — any compact object the decryption procedure requires may be unique, shared, or universal and is expected to propagate through the swarm (P12). 
  **(B) is accepted as sufficient.** **(A)'s prerequisites are available:** the NFT ownership history is an authenticated seller-to-buyer chain; the NFT may carry a link, reference, or object enabling programmatic behavior (P02, S08); the original issuer may be on the decryption pathway provided it has no selective deniability (R08 clarification, P11). 
  *Operational criterion for (B) — accepted by the user on 2026-09-16 as sufficient if it yields a valid method, not as obligate (log 15); our deduction, S15:* (i) conforming decryption consumes the holder's identity-bound private material for every piece and produces no reusable common intermediate; (ii) every **compact** decryption-equivalent object a holder can derive either reveals that holder's identity secret or is traceable to the holder; (iii) payload-proportional derived objects (per-piece key files) and non-secret public helpers are acceptable. Clause (ii) is what separates (B) from a wrapped common key (X02): without it, distinct keys that compute one mask are labels on a common key. Clause (ii) is the non-convertibility property of traceable-PRF keys and the non-strippable, non-bearer witness property for witness encryption, so **(A) and (B) share their mathematical core**: the weak form of tracing — issued objects identify their holder — is part of R04's content, while arbitrary-decoder tracing (P06) remains optional. The user acknowledged on 2026-09-16 that clause (ii) is a pathway to traitor tracing (log 15); no clause is obligate, so failing one is a recorded property rather than a disqualification. 
  **2026-09-21 review:** the claim that (A) and (B) necessarily have the same mathematical core is withdrawn. Log 15 makes this a sufficient stronger route, not a definition of every acceptable route. Fresh native keys with ledger-bound commitments may satisfy issued distinctness without self-enforcement or tracing. C11 evaluates that route explicitly. The claim that only the named multi-key-mask class can satisfy (A) was not established as a theorem.
3. **Deletion and persistent credentials.** A wallet identity key may persist. A retained asset decryption root that can generate every new window key is a different capability. Identify it explicitly instead of claiming that erasing only a leaf key destroys all decryption capability. Whether that lifecycle is acceptable is a candidate-specific application decision, not a settled mathematical impossibility.
4. **Helper budget.** Small fraction is qualitative. Measure total persistent bytes, per-holder bytes, per-window bytes, per-transfer bytes, and accumulation over time. A short update token does not imply that the updated ciphertext or helper is short.
5. **Meaning of an attempt.** Piece, file, stream session, and retry boundaries need an eventual protocol definition. Finality, stale state, reorganization handling, and offline behavior must be explicit; a cached historical proof is not proof of current ownership.
6. **Transfer participation and recovery.** A helper produced by the seller may fit an ordinary voluntary sale. Forced transfers, lost keys, account recovery, and administrative transitions may require a different path. Establish the concrete cryptographic dependency before asking which application cases must be supported. *User decision, 2026-09-16 (P13):* forced transfer, lost keys, and recovery are not protocol obligations. The seller's participation in a voluntary sale and the publisher's at mint are accepted dependencies when made non-deniable (R08 clarification). What remains open here is administrative transition — First Finder and escrow claim (R12) — and the atomic, publicly verifiable delivery of any per-holder material at settlement.
7. **Loss of decryption ability at transfer (user statement 2026-09-21, log 22).** The user states that a bearer must lose the ability to decrypt the volume when possession of the entitlement transfers, and that retaining previously decrypted plaintext is not the same as retaining the entitlement to decrypt again. Three readings fit the words. 
  **(a) Conforming-protocol loss:** the entitlement and protocol authorization end at transfer and the conforming client destroys its material (R06); a modified prior bearer that kept complete inputs is the accepted S01 boundary (log 9, P05). C09, C10 and C11 all satisfy this. **(a′) Protocol-invalid and distinguishable stale material:** in addition, the prior bearer's material must not be the same object the new bearer holds, so the protocol can name the authorized credential of the open interval and treat the old one as stale. C10 and C11 satisfy this through path growth or fresh randomization bound by the interval record; C09's shared bearer key does not. 
  **(b) Cryptographic loss against a modified prior bearer:** the retained material must stop working. For a fixed common ciphertext this contradicts S01 and log 9: the prior bearer keeps compatible capsules and a valid key. Reaching (b) would require re-encrypting the body at every transfer (against R01), a fresh secret-bearing component on every read (R07, X12), or hardware (X14); none is available under the unchanged requirements. *User decision, 2026-09-21 (log 24):* the three are an escalation chain, not alternatives. 
  **(a) is the minimum viable construction**, depending on protocol and client conformance. **(a′) is potentially available and indeterminate**; it improves the protocol while still relying on client conformance, and is the working target. 
  **(b) is the preference** but does not appear available under the existing constraints — the common ciphertext (R01) and the rejection of trusted hardware (P09) among them — and is recorded as P15. C11 meets (a′); C09 meets only (a). If a fresh secret bearing component on every read can be constructed without publisher deniability, that would appear to suffice for reaching (b) while preserving the constraints requiring a common ciphertext and refusing a TEE. It is possible that a fresh secret bearing component on every read could be accomplished through the involvement of some method on the NFT in the decryption chain. Involving the publisher in the decryption path, even when undeniable, introduces latency and risk that the publisher is not online, which produces deniability due to lack of availablility for service, so is indistinguishable from selective deniability, but if the method is on the NFT, and the bearer client must be online anyway for the decryption path, the latency and availability risk may be manageable. 
  *Analysis of the user's note (2026-09-21, log 25; our deduction):* a contract method executes in public and cannot itself hold or generate a secret (S03, S08); any value it emits is copyable (X06). What a chain method *can* supply is a fresh public **attestation** — that at epoch `e` the owner of `k` is `pk` — while confidentiality rides on the holder's private key. Reaching (b) with that ingredient needs two things: capsules that accept only the current epoch's attestation, which for a fixed ciphertext means pre-encrypting one capsule set per epoch over the deployment's horizon (about `E` times the sidecar — roughly 120 monthly or 520 weekly epochs over ten years, our arithmetic), and a decryption relation that consumes the public attestation together with the holder's private key — exactly C02's private-witness relation with an epoch term, subject to its open formability and true-statement-security checks (E45 gives false-statement security only). 
  A stale attestation must not open a later epoch's capsule; the attestation is public, so it supplies freshness, never confidentiality. No secret-bearing party acts at read time, so S20 is respected. This is the only route to (b) visible under the unchanged constraints and is recorded under C02 as the (b)-route; C11 supplies (a′) now and is a candidate for the holder-key half of such a relation.

“Encrypt with the user's private key” is not a complete confidentiality primitive. Standard public-key encryption encrypts to a recipient's public key and decrypts with its private key; signatures prove control but are normally public. Any alternative use needs an actual scheme and security definition.

## Backward chain from the observable target

1. **Observable result:** an entitled holder decrypts any requested piece of the common payload to its original bytes, using distinct material assigned to the current entitlement window.
2. **Decryption interface:** conceptually, `Decrypt(C, pieceIndex, D_window, H_optional)` returns the piece. Here `D_window` is secret native material and `H_optional` is a permitted compact helper. This notation specifies an obligation, not an implemented cipher.
3. **Payload compatibility:** every valid window's material must work with that same `C`. The construction must explain this algebraically without simply recovering the old common SCK. This is one unresolved seam.
4. **Authorization binding:** the material and helper must bind asset, deployment, exact entitlement, ownership interval, holder identity, and the selected window context. Include settlement reference, expiry bounds, and freshness identifiers where that window policy uses them. Bind a key-agreement or ephemeral public key when the particular construction uses one.
5. **Private input:** only the authorized holder can supply the required private witness or secret operation. The ledger supplies authenticated entitlement state, not this confidentiality by itself.
6. **Resolution without threshold release:** either the holder's private witness directly drives a suitable encryption primitive, public updates adapt a compact helper, or an authorized secret participant creates a narrowly delegated helper at an allowed lifecycle event. Identify which.
7. **Future-state coverage:** the ciphertext and any persistent public parameters must support later owners and newly minted entitlements, including escrow, without having listed those keys when encrypting the payload. This is the other recurring unresolved seam.
8. **Operational lifecycle:** before each attempt, the conforming client checks current state and wallet control. It destroys expired window material and does not treat retained material as a right. Specify finality, races, helper availability, devices, and recovery.
9. **Established components:** ordinary signatures, public-key encryption, commitments, ledger proofs, and several specialized encryption/delegation primitives exist. A complete protocol must prove their composition and measure its costs. A diagram of components is not that proof.

The present investigation belongs primarily at steps 3, 6, and 7. Steps 4, 5, and 8 still need precise composition, but revisiting the accepted limitation of step 8 does not solve the missing primitives.

## Candidate register

Statuses describe the evidence accumulated in this investigation, not a claim about every paper in a field. **Open** means a concrete proof or construction obligation remains. **Conditional** means the path needs an identified lifecycle or requirement choice. **Parked** means it is not on the current critical path. **Excluded instance** rejects only the described construction.

### C01 — Direct private-witness decryption

**Status: open; strongest conceptual fit to avoiding a standing secret-release service.**

Proposed shape: the holder creates a fresh private window secret or keypair. Wallet-authorized state binds its public commitment/key to a valid NFT context. Decryption uses that private witness together with authenticated chain evidence. The published encryption policy recognizes valid future grants, rather than naming only a fixed holder at publication.

The window secret should not be deterministically regenerable from the permanent wallet key if honest erasure is intended to remove that capability. A private decryption signature must be distinct from the signature or proof broadcast with the registration transaction.

The Nuta–Schuldt–Nishide blockchain construction provides a useful precedent: a fresh private ephemeral value is committed in a signed blockchain record and used as a witness, with erasure after use. It assumes extractable witness encryption. Its recipient key is fixed at encryption and its first-record mechanism is not already a reusable, transferable NFT access policy. [E01]

**Open obligations:** a secure relation accepting unknown future NFT holders; authentic chain continuation and finality; private-witness security for true statements; practical encryption and piece decryption; dynamic minting; First Finder; and compatibility with the canonical payload. Encrypting only the SCK under witness encryption solves neither the native-key divergence nor SCK extractability. Direct payload encryption or another demonstrated payload construction is needed.

**Cost evidence:** one general-purpose 2026 proposal estimates hundreds of terabytes of ciphertext for 100-bit security. This is that proposal's estimate, not a lower bound on witness encryption or on specialized NFT relations. [E02]

### C02 — Specialized signature, commitment, or blockchain witness encryption

Do not turn an unverified arithmetic-circuit WE claim into either a solution or a refutation. The IEEE paper linked in E09 was accessible only at abstract level during this investigation.

**Candidate relation sharpened by Q01 (2026-09-16; qualified 2026-09-21).** One possible route is witness encryption for `R((vk_cons, k), (pk, σ, sk)) := Verify(vk_cons, k‖pk, σ) ∧ pk = g^sk`, where `vk_cons` is the consensus certification key, `k` the asset or entitlement identifier, `pk` the holder's registered key, `σ` the chain's certification of that registration, and `sk` the private component. The encryptor knows only `(vk_cons, k)`; observers know `(pk, σ)`; confidentiality must hold against them. Two checks precede any gadget work:

1. *Ciphertext formability.* With a random-oracle message hash (BLS as in E04) the encryptor cannot form the ciphertext, because `H(k‖pk)` is unknown at encryption. The certification must therefore be algebraic in `pk` (a structure-preserving or Waters-type signature over a group-element message) or the WE must be proof-based (E06, E09). The consensus key must also be stable across validator rotation or certified by a chain of keys the ciphertext can follow; that is a separate obligation.
2. *Public-key substitution hazard.* In pairing-linear gadgets the decryptor's private-scalar term has the form `X^sk` with `X ∈ G_T`. Whenever the observer can obtain a `G_2` element `Z` with `X = e(g_1, Z)` from the projection key, the signature, or the public parameters, then `X^sk = e(pk, Z)` is computable from the public key alone and `sk` is not a private witness. For each candidate gadget, show that no such `Z` lies in the observer's span — including elements formed by pairing signature components that contain `pk` raised to a signing-key power. This derivation is ours and is unverified against the E06 gadgets.
3. *Strippability and bearer witnesses (added after the 2026-09-16 decisions).* If the certification binds `pk` algebraically, check whether the holder can cancel the `pk`-dependence with `sk` — for example cancellation in a same-group construction with an available `g^s` — and obtain a common witness. In a type-3 pairing, if σ lies in G1 and vk in G2, `σ / vk^sk` is undefined; an efficient cross-group map cannot be assumed. If the WE decrypts with a proof or a signature as its effective witness, that object is a bearer object usable without `sk`: in proof-based WE (E06) any valid proof for the fixed statement decrypts. Both may fail the optional stronger clause (ii), not issued distinctness itself. A private bearer proof is not automatically public; its copyability alone is not an outsider-confidentiality break.

The former **formability–substitution–bearer trilemma** is a heuristic for the stronger identity-bound branch, not a theorem or a gate on all acceptable constructions. The substitution test needs the specified public preimage Z; not every pairing-linear term necessarily exposes one. Formability, typed group operations and true-statement/private-witness security remain concrete checks for Q02. The new E45 preprint supplies only false-statement security and does not close that particular gap.

**The (b)-route (2026-09-21, log 25).** Interpretation 7's preferred reading maps onto this candidate: per-epoch pre-encrypted capsule sets whose relation consumes a public consensus attestation of the current owner for that epoch together with the holder's private key. Costs are `E` sidecars over the deployment horizon plus the relation's own ciphertext; the cryptographic gaps are unchanged — formability without the future holder's key, non-strippability, true-statement security — and the attestation supplies only freshness. Recorded so that (b) is pursued here rather than as a contract "method" that would have to hold a secret (S03, S08). The `E` capsule-set cost is not removable by puncturable or forward-secure keys (X21).

### C03 — Registration-based encryption and public compact updates

**Status: excluded as an independent access path (2026-09-16); retained as a component reference. See S11-S14 and X18. The 2026-09-15 status was "open; promising helper path".**

Users generate their own keys; a public curator aggregates registration information and supplies public helper updates without holding a master decryption secret. This directly addresses one source of issuer trust. An implemented registration-based encryption construction reports 914-byte ciphertexts and millisecond encryption/decryption. These are scheme-specific figures, not an NFT payload benchmark. [E10]

Existing registered-ABE correctness reviewed here applies to ciphertext encrypted under parameters that include the registration. It does not automatically give a newly registered user access to an old ciphertext. A changing public key, changing ciphertext header, and changing user helper are three different operations. [E11]

**Open obligations:** public adaptation for future registrations while retaining the common payload; who can create that adaptation; native distinct material rather than a shared-key KEM; bounded helper costs; transfer and repeated windows; dynamic issuance; First Finder; and asset-wide deployment rights.

**Leads dispositioned 2026-09-16** (the 2026-09-15 entry listed these as unfinished): Cuckoo Commitments is a compiler from small- to large-identity RBE and inherits post-registration encryption [E30]; "updatable RBE" is not a re-targeting notion — in RBE an update is auxiliary information the decryptor fetches after later registrations, and its count has a proven lower bound [E28, E29]; the revocable registered-ABE result removes access by deregistration and does not grant access to earlier ciphertexts [E32]; the linear-CRS registered ABE of GLWW and of the E06 framework keep the post-registration model [E06, E12]; the distributed-broadcast lead is a CCA-security result in which the sender fixes the recipient subset from existing public keys [E31]; the lattice RBE registry that supports key insert, remove, and update describes registry operations, not access to ciphertexts created before the key existed [E33]. All dispositions except E28 are abstract-level. Retroactive access without a secret author is, by S12, witness encryption for the certification relation and is tracked under C02/Q02. Delivery of authored material to a known recipient needs only public-key encryption (S14). The family's remaining value here is evidence that a public curator is compatible with self-generated keys, and the E06 gadget framework itself.

### C04 — Compact proxy re-encryption or delegated helper

**Status: conditional/open; plausible placement of secret work at publication or transfer.**

Proposed shape: a secret-bearing party creates a compact delegation or transformation bound to the new holder's public key and NFT context. A proxy need not see plaintext; the NFT authenticates the helper or its hash. The holder uses the helper and its own native secret while checking current state.

The critical distinction is whether the helper transforms a small cryptographic header or requires a personalized copy of the payload. A compact re-encryption key can still be used to transform an entire file. A hybrid PRE system that just rewraps the common SCK remains the baseline divergence.

**Concrete counterexample already found:** in the examined AFGH construction, a recipient with the re-encryption value `g^(a1*b2)` and its secret `b2` can recover `g^a1`. Combined with the original pairing ciphertext, that value is a common weak decryption capability. This is within the paper's distinction between weak decryption secrets and the delegator's full master secret; it is not a break of its stated master-key security. It excludes that use as a solution to the stronger native-material requirement, not every PRE scheme. [E13]

**Open obligations:** collusion-resistant and repeatable delegation; secret/helper author; atomic buyer readiness; later minting; unavailable or lost seller; recovery; First Finder and escrow claim; helper availability; and whether any retained ancestor key decrypts or regenerates all future keys. Binding a helper update into a sale is not enough unless its correctness and buyer usability are established before settlement. Public consensus can verify supplied evidence; it cannot supply an absent seller's secret transformation by itself.

*2026-09-16 decisions applied (wording fixed 2026-09-20, log 15):* seller participation in a voluntary sale and publisher participation at mint are accepted dependencies (P13; R08 clarification). "Seller" in this candidate is the previous entitlement holder who authors the buyer's helper at a voluntary sale — never the publisher; the "unavailable or lost seller" obligation (that previous holder cannot participate: forced transfer, lost key, absence) and "recovery" leave the list under P13. The publisher's discretion over new issuance is unaffected (R10) and its discretion over an existing entitlement's transfer is forbidden (R09). The atomic, publicly verifiable helper-at-settlement requirement above is now the non-deniability mechanism rather than an extra. X10 was re-checked against the (B) criterion: it also fails clause (ii) (compact, identity-free recovered value); the original exclusion ground stands. Under that criterion a delegated helper should be usable only with the buyer's identity-bound secret and should not let the buyer derive an identity-free compact decrypter; the clauses are sufficient, not obligate (log 15).

ERC-7857 is an interface/example lead, not a verified solution. The inspected version described ZK work as unfinished and included TEE-dependent behavior. Do not infer a trust-free construction from the presence of an NFT transfer method. [E14]

### C05 — Key-homomorphic, updatable, or constrained-function helpers

**Status: reviewed instances deprioritized; C11 now supplies a simpler native-key route.**

Prior source checks, consolidated 2026-09-21: E15's Section 5.4 changes a header while preserving a symmetric-key-encrypted body, and its recipient recovers that symmetric key. E16's compactness definition concerns header/token size, not unchanged payload bytes; its chained example encrypts the preceding body again. E17's packed LWE construction updates the body, has a bounded update budget, and its polynomial-matrix tokens scale with the packing parameter. These facts do not prove that every key-homomorphic or updatable scheme fails; they explain why these instances did not advance the preferred compact unchanged-body construction. Do not repeat their initial survey.

The question is whether a compact constrained transformation can connect independent holder secrets to the same payload without revealing a reusable common asset key. Relevant literature includes key-homomorphic constrained PRFs and updatable encryption. [E15-E17]

Simple public key deltas do not solve the problem: a holder with its key and an invertible delta can recover the shared original key. Constraining a delta might reduce what can be recovered, but the asset-level decryption capability and collusion behavior still need analysis.

**Open obligations:** exact transformation equations; security under holder/helper collusion; whether the body changes; whether a header is enough; all sizes; who computes update tokens; and whether the result is truly native distinct decryption or a renamed wrapping layer.

### C06 — Delegatable identity/attribute encryption and local window derivation

**Status: conditional/open lead (2026-09-15); 2026-09-20: its wildcard and hierarchical leads feed C10, and the delegation objection below dissolves under the changed premise (S17, S01).**

Wildcard identity encryption, hierarchical delegation, ABE key randomization, and decentralized authorization systems may support locally derived or restricted keys. They deserve examination now that tracing and modified-client expiry are not mandatory. [E18-E19]

However, a key fixed to one owner generally cannot delegate to a different owner merely by filling additional wildcard positions. Keeping an ancestor credential may retain asset-wide decryption or issuance power. A publisher-issued attribute key also needs an issuance/escrow story.

*2026-09-20:* the objection above assumed the old owner must lose capability when the new owner gains it. S01 accepts retention, and log 14-15 accept the seller as the delegating party at a voluntary sale, so the seller deriving the buyer's child key *is* the transfer. An ancestor's retained power over sub-identities of the same entitlement equals leaking that entitlement's access (S01), not asset-wide power. Publisher issuance at mint is now permitted (R08, R10). See C10.

**Open obligations:** common-ciphertext compatibility; changing owner without transferring the same capability; unbounded transfer depth; native window isolation; persistent-secret accounting; dynamic minting; and whether the cited system encrypts only authorization metadata rather than this payload.

### C07 — Traceable PRFs, traitor tracing, and functional encryption

**Status: parked as a secondary enhancement (2026-09-15); 2026-09-20: partially unparked into C09 — traitor-tracing KEMs are relevant as master-issued, post-encryption, collusion-resistant key generation with per-piece headers, not for decoder tracing (S17).**

Traceable evaluation keys can differ while computing a common pseudorandom mask, giving a conceptual direct-payload route. Existing candidates still need issuer, collusion, cost, and dynamic-recipient analysis. A reviewed older lattice traceable-PRF scheme exposes its master key under two-key collusion; a subsequent construction changes the security and parameter tradeoff. This is a scheme-specific result, not a rejection of all traceable PRFs. [E20-E21]

Pairing-based optimal traitor tracing and registered functional encryption are adjacent results. Do not mistake a traceable KEM for native payload decryption if every recipient obtains the same content key. Do not resume arbitrary-decoder tracing work unless it helps a primary requirement or the user restores tracing to the critical path. [E22-E23]

### C08 — Delegatable multi-key mask along the ownership chain

**Status: optional stronger branch; native issued distinctness now has the concrete C11 route.**

The assertions below about exact full-domain equality and the smallest common decrypter were research targets, not established traceable-PRF guarantees. The reviewed traceable-PRF notion allows negligible disagreement on random inputs; that is not automatically correctness on every sequential piece index. No general compactness lower bound or mandatory tracing requirement is established.

Shape: the body is masked piece by piece by a function that many distinct full-domain keys compute identically, so the smallest common decryption-equivalent object is the mask itself, payload-sized. The publisher authors the first key at mint; at a voluntary transfer the seller derives a fresh, distinct key for the buyer, so neither the publisher nor a network is on the transfer or read path. Under clause (ii) of the proposed (B) criterion the keys must be non-convertible — a holder or coalition cannot derive an identity-free compact key — which is the traceable-PRF property (S15). Without it the distinct keys are labels on a common key and C08 collapses to X02.

Required primitive: a PRF or mask family with (1) many distinct keys computing one function, (2) parent-to-child delegation producing a fresh distinct key, (3) no compact common object derivable by any coalition, and (4) per-piece evaluation cheap enough for seekable reads. Traceable PRFs give (1) and bounded-collusion (3) with a master-key issuer and without (2) [E20, E21]; hierarchical delegation gives (2) for encryption keys, not for a common mask [E18]. Whether a delegatable traceable PRF or an equivalent exists is unsearched (Q07).

**Open obligations (2026-09-16 decisions applied):** framing — the parent must not be able to reproduce the child's exact key, so key generation at a sale must be a two-party step in which the buyer contributes private randomness; atomic, publicly verifiable delivery of the buyer's key material with settlement, so the seller has no selective deniability; First Finder and escrow claim still need an ancestor-key holder (R12, R14); growth of delegation depth over many transfers; coalition analysis across the ownership chain; all sizes. Forced transfer, lost keys, and recovery are no longer obligations (P13). The NFT ownership history serves as the authenticated record of the delegation chain, and the NFT may reference each holder's public helper (P02, P12).

### C09 — Master-issued per-entitlement keys, bearer-transferred with the NFT

**Status: derived 2026-09-20 under the changed premise (log 14-15, S17); instance verification is Q08. Fallback for C10.**

Shape: at publication the publisher runs the setup of a scheme in which any key issued later from the master secret decrypts any ciphertext under the public parameters, and encrypts the body as one header per piece (or per group of pieces) encapsulating a per-piece key, with the piece under that key. At mint — the publisher's discretionary act (R10) — it issues `key_i` for entitlement `i` and delivers it to the first owner's key-agreement key atomically with the mint. At a voluntary sale the seller delivers `key_i` to the buyer's key-agreement key atomically with settlement; the buyer checks the key against the public parameters and against the entitlement's published binding before signing. Reads are local (P11); the conforming client checks state per attempt (R05) and destroys the key on transfer (R06).

Instances (all unverified for this composition): BGW05 broadcast encryption with every slot as the recipient set — two-element headers and keys, public parameters linear in the slot count, slot count fixed at setup [E35] (excluded 2026-09-21 by X19: the user requires no pre-bound entitlement count); wildcard IBE encrypted to the all-wildcard pattern — unbounded identities, header growth per pattern level scheme-specific [E34]; traitor-tracing KEMs, which add decoder tracing and, in some schemes, public traceability [E22, E37].

Properties: keys are distinct per entitlement but shared along that entitlement's ownership chain. This does not supply a distinct key for each ownership interval. Reads and voluntary bearer transfers need no publisher or provisioner; current-state validation still requires a fresh state view. The per-piece hybrid exposes common piece keys, so the earlier claim of no compact common object outside the publisher is withdrawn. The optional stronger clauses (i)-(iii) are not proved merely by carrying an identity index. C11 supplies a native-key-changing alternative.

**Open obligations:** native per-window material, verifiable delivery, setup-bound supply for BGW, implementation costs, and First Finder/claim custody. Publishing a successor and reissuing to known holders is not itself an escrow solution: the original deployment remains live, pre-claim issuance must work, and no holder may be stranded. See the comparison and custody analysis below. A same-parameter successor is compatible with existing valid keys; changing parameters requires an explicit migration construction. Attribution to a chain is optional and does not substitute for R04.

### C10 — Hierarchical delegation along the ownership chain (per-interval keys)

**Status: derived 2026-09-20 from C06's leads under the changed premise (S17); matching semantics verified from the WIBE text [E34]; instance costs and asymmetric derivation unverified (Q08).**

Shape: the body is encrypted, piece by piece, to the all-wildcard pattern `(*, …, *)` of depth `L` in a wildcard identity-based encryption scheme, whose key derivation is exactly that of a hierarchical IBE [E34]. The publisher derives the depth-1 key for identity `(i)` at mint. At each voluntary sale the seller derives the child key `(i, …, buyer)` from its own key and delivers it atomically with settlement. In WIBE an identity matches the pattern when it is no longer than the pattern and agrees with it at every non-wildcard position, and the paper states that any ancestor of a matching identity also matches because it can derive the descendant's key [E34]; so every owner along the chain, at any depth up to `L`, decrypts the same ciphertext with a distinct key that embeds its path.

Attribution: a leaked key names its path, hence the owners up to that depth — later owners are excluded, earlier ones are not. **Non-repudiation against ancestors is not available inside WIBE (S18):** because the ciphertext is all-wildcard, any ancestor can derive a usable key for *any* descendant identity by filling the wildcard with a value of its choice, so a leaked descendant key could always have been made by an ancestor. Using the buyer's wallet secret as its identity component does not repair this — the buyer would then need every ancestor's secret to decrypt, and ancestors can still fill the level arbitrarily. Self-enforcement therefore needs a mechanism outside HIBE key derivation; asymmetric traitor tracing and self-enforcement [E36] remain the literature to check for one. Publisher framing is unchanged: the master-secret holder can derive any key.

Properties: per-interval distinct native material used per piece (clauses i-iii); no provisioner, network, or publisher on the read or transfer path; publisher discretion only at mint; unbounded entitlements, since identities are strings; the seller's retained parent key decrypts forever (S01) and can derive further sub-keys of the same entitlement only, which equals leaking that entitlement's access. Falls back to C09 bearer transfer when the depth bound is exhausted.

**Q08 review correction (2026-09-21):** the full E34 PDF and supplied text are now readable. BB-WIBE uses BDDH; BBG-WIBE uses the stronger decisional L-BDHI assumption, not BDDH. The selective-pattern theorems do not need the random-oracle upgrade used for adaptive patterns. A single fixed public pattern is an appropriate selective-pattern application, but it does not extend the identity domain. Setup fixes maximum depth L. At the all-wildcard pattern of depth L, every legal identity matches, and the security game allows no legal nonmatching-key queries. The former assertion that it protects against holders of keys deeper than L is withdrawn: those keys are outside this setup. This is outsider confidentiality, not revocation of formerly valid keys.

**Remaining obligations:** an implementation and group choice supporting the stated assumptions; verifiable encrypted-key delivery and header authentication; First Finder custody; and the exact window/storage protocol. Prefix attribution and non-repudiation are optional properties, not reasons to block the primary construction. R11 works for correctly formed ciphertexts under the same parameters; incompatible-parameter redeployment needs a separate compatibility mechanism. C11 removes the need to increase depth for every new holder.

The identity domains are finite cryptographic domains, not literally infinite supplies. WIBE avoids a separately configured recipient count; a collision-resistant encoding of registry identifiers must still be specified. BB-WIBE keys contain d+1 elements. BBG keys include L−d remaining delegation components and two decryption elements; Figure 1's L+2 is the maximum, not the depth-L leaf size.

If a capsule takes q bytes and covers B payload bytes, its common-sidecar overhead is approximately q/B; total capsule storage is `ceil(payloadBytes/B)*q`. A 1 MiB group makes that fraction `q/1048576`; a 16 KiB piece makes it `q/16384`. This is a size formula, not a claim that pairing latency, serialization, proof costs or small-object economics have been measured. Holder-envelope storage is O(number of recorded windows), with constant credential size per C11a window.

**Conclusion of the comparison:** C11a obtains fresh interval keys at C09's depth-one ciphertext and decryption cost, avoiding C10's finite ownership-path depth. C10 remains relevant if an embedded path is valuable, but no longer needs to be selected merely to get distinct native keys. BBG trades a stronger parameterized assumption and delegation-key storage for fewer pairings at larger depth. BGW retains a setup-bound slot count. None of these facts settles the custody or storage policy.

### C11 — Fixed-depth rerandomization of native entitlement keys

**Status: algebra verified 2026-09-21; proposed protocol composition, not an adopted suite.** This is a third choice beyond C09's bearer transfer and C10's growing hierarchy. It uses the same BB-WIBE construction [E34], at depth one, but changes the native key at each ownership interval without changing the identity encrypted to. The NFT binds the issued key to its holder and interval. The distinction matters: identity-path growth is not necessary for distinct native key material.

**C11a: same-identity BB-WIBE.** In the paper's symmetric prime-order pairing groups, public parameters are `g1, g2, h1 = g1^alpha, u0, u1`; master issuance material is `h2 = g2^alpha`. Map the exact entitlement identifier to a scalar `I` with an authenticated, domain-separated mapping. Define `F_I = u0 * u1^I`. At mint the issuer samples a secret `r` and issues only the group-element tuple `D = (A, B) = (h2 * F_I^r, g1^r)`. It does not issue `r`.

For the all-wildcard pattern `(*)`, encrypt a random group message `M_j` with fresh `t_j` as `C_j = (U_j, V_j, W_j, Z_j) = (g1^t_j, u0^t_j, u1^t_j, M_j * e(h1,g2)^t_j)`. Every valid entitlement key recovers exactly the same message: `M_j = Z_j * e(B, V_j * W_j^I) / e(U_j, A)`. The numerator cancels the entitlement-specific blinding factor in the denominator. These are the depth-one specialization of the paper's equations, not a new confidentiality assumption.

At transfer the seller samples a fresh private offset `s` and computes `D_new = (A * F_I^s, B * g1^s)`. This is a key with total exponent `r+s`, exactly the fresh-key distribution for the same `I`. It requires neither `r` nor the master secret. Size remains two source group elements after any polynomially bounded number of transfers; there is no transfer-depth parameter. Fresh samples give distinct keys except with negligible collision probability. A buyer may privately rerandomize again after delivery, then bind the final commitment to its interval.

**Critical secret handling:** the seller sends the group tuple, never the total randomizer. A holder who learns `r` can compute `h2 = A / F_I^r`. Knowing its own additional offset `s` does not reveal the unknown original total. Private buyer randomization prevents a seller from simply reconstructing that exact final tuple, but supplies no tracing or non-repudiation theorem. A prior holder retains mathematical access if it keeps its old tuple, as already accepted in S01.

**Public-parameter validity check.** For a secret candidate tuple, the holder can check `e(A,g1) = e(h1,g2) * e(F_I,B)`, together with canonical encoding, curve and subgroup validation. In the prime-order model, writing `B=g1^r` and using nondegeneracy shows that this equation forces `A=h2*F_I^r`; the verifier need not know `r`. The tuple must remain secret. Publishing a proof of this check requires zero knowledge; publishing the tuple itself gives observers access.

**Independent re-derivation (2026-09-21, second maintainer).** Decryption correctness of C11a and C11c, the fresh-key distribution of `(A·F^s, B·g1^s)`, and the Q09 substitution `T = P^(r_A+s_A) · R^(-r_B-s_B)` were re-derived by hand and agree with the text above. Two further properties follow from the same algebra and are recorded as our deductions: (1) master-secret hiding — recovering `h2` from any number of valid tuples `(h2·F^r_i, g1^r_i)` requires `F^r_i` from `g1^r_i`, a CDH instance; no holder ever learns its own total exponent, so neither a tuple holder nor a coalition of them can strip a key, and a seller's known offset `s` gives no advantage; (2) in C11a a tuple for identity `I` cannot be converted into a tuple for `I' ≠ I` without `u1^r`, again CDH from `B`, and the validity equation identifies the unique `I` a usable tuple was issued for. So C11a supplies entitlement-level attribution of a usable leaked tuple with non-convertibility across entitlements, while C11c supplies neither; both leave interval attribution optional (S19). Under interpretation 7, C11 satisfies readings (a) and (a′), not (b).

**C11b: same-attribute CP-ABE.** Waters' standard-model construction [E40] is another exact rerandomization instance. Use one asset attribute and one public parameter set per asset. Its key `(K,L,K_a) = (g^alpha * (g^a)^t, g^t, h_a^t)` becomes `(K*(g^a)^u, L*g^u, K_a*h_a^u)` using public bases and fresh offset `u`. Key size is three source-group elements with no transfer-depth growth. The original `t` must not be disclosed. Public-parameter checks are `e(K,g)=e(g,g)^alpha*e(g^a,L)` and `e(K_a,g)=e(h_a,L)`. The paper proves selective security under its stated q-parallel BDHE assumption; its symmetric-group proof is not automatically a proof for a type-3 implementation. Our NFT composition is separate from that theorem. BSW CP-ABE has an explicit Delegate algorithm [E41], but its original generic-group/random-oracle security model is a less attractive implementation basis.

Unlike C11a's fixed entitlement identity, C11b's cryptographic attribute identifies the asset. Every holder can create another working same-attribute tuple; only authorized ledger issuance creates a new legal entitlement. This can simplify credential continuity after an administrative claim, but an available secret-bearing participant is still required to initialize a new holder.

**C11c: a fixed asset identity with independently randomized credentials.** Use one fixed public identity `I_asset` for every credential of an asset, with independent parameters for different assets. Encrypt to that fixed identity. The secret tuple remains `(A,B)=(h2*F^r,g1^r)`, with `F=F_I_asset`; rerandomization remains `(A*F^s,B*g1^s)`. The capsule is `(U,V,Z)=(g1^t,F^t,M*Y^t)` and decryption is `M=Z*e(B,V)/e(U,A)`. This is the fixed-identity specialization of E34: a `2bG+bT` capsule, `2bG` native key and two basic decryption pairings, with no supply or transfer-depth parameter.

Each NFT/window gets an independently randomized native tuple and authenticated encrypted delivery. NFT and holder identities are bound by the ledger/protocol, not by different cryptographic identity strings inside those tuples. Cryptographic key generation creates no legal minting authority. A holder can make more working tuples, just as a modified holder could already share access; only the authorized publisher or escrow contract can create a valid NFT. Strict cryptographic separation between NFTs is not supplied. C11a retains the variant with an embedded entitlement identity.

C11c gives the holder-assisted issuance benefit of C11b with two key elements rather than three. Any existing compatible credential can generate a fresh tuple for a **ledger-authorized** recipient. A publisher, First Finder custodian or claimant may retain such an issuance credential; it can generate fresh tuples without knowing h2 or their total randomizers. Public successor encryption also needs no h2. Recovering the original master secret is therefore unnecessary for protocol-level minting/publishing continuity in this variant. An available secret compatible credential is still necessary; this is not public secret-free issuance. Never publish the discrete logarithm of F relative to g1: together with B, that logarithm strips the key's blinding and reveals h2. Likewise, g2 must not equal g1 or have a publicly known discrete logarithm relative to it: that would expose h2 from the public h1. Independently sampled source-group generators in the source construction must not be replaced with publicly known scalar multiples merely for implementation convenience.

**Common payload and seekability.** Our proposed hybrid uses independent random `M_j` values per piece, derives a symmetric piece key with a domain-separated KDF, and encrypts that piece once. Every holder receives the same encrypted pieces and the same authenticated capsule sidecar. Bind asset, deployment, suite, piece index and geometry in the key derivation/authentication context. No per-holder payload copy or payload-sized helper is produced. A holder-specific encrypted credential is constant-size before transport, proof and context overhead, and can be NFT-linked in the swarm. The all-wildcard header contains three source-group elements and one target-group element; do not call all four 48-byte elements.

This exposes common derived per-piece keys. It does not issue a deployment-wide SCK, and the native credential remains a distinct algebraic decryption key rather than a fresh transport wrapper around that SCK. It fits reading (B) as recorded in log 14-15; it does not meet a stronger prohibition on all common derived material. A collection of piece keys is a reusable small decrypter for that deployment. The optional stronger criterion is not claimed. Grouping pieces reduces capsule/pairing cost but increases how much content one derived key unlocks; it is a published suite choice, not a hidden cache.

**Mint, transfer and use — proposed composition.**

1. At publication the publisher retains asset issuance material, publishes authenticated parameters, and encrypts the common payload and sidecar. First Finder uses the separate custody analysis below.
2. At mint, the publisher authors a valid randomized tuple for the exact NFT. At voluntary transfer, the current holder rerandomizes its tuple. The recipient supplies an encryption public key authenticated by its wallet; the author encrypts the tuple to that key.
3. The settlement record binds the ciphertext/helper digest and a hiding commitment to the tuple, asset, exact NFT, ownership-interval counter, recipient identity/key, suite and chosen window context. Prove that the encrypted tuple opens that commitment and satisfies the validity equation. The witness consists of group elements, encryption coins and commitment opening, **not** the unknown key randomizer. Contract checks of issuance authority/ownership are separate predicates.
4. A transition succeeds only with a valid, available encrypted credential for the buyer. The proof relation is specified; the concrete proof/encryption composition, setup and cost remain Q09. General NIZKs and pairing-equation proof systems exist [E42], but that does not make an arbitrary wallet encryption scheme directly compatible with a particular proof system.
5. For every conforming attempt, validate the current settled NFT owner, issuance lineage, wallet control, interval/window and authenticated payload descriptor, then use the tuple locally. Expiry invalidates protocol use and triggers destruction of in-memory keys, derivatives and contexts. Reading does not contact the publisher, seller or a threshold provisioner; checking current consensus state still needs a fresh state view.
6. The current credential/encrypted helper is persistent for an ownership interval if restart and subsequent voluntary transfer are to work. This is a disclosed change from the MVP's memory-only provisioned responses. Historical helpers may remain in the swarm. Conforming software must not unwrap or use them after entitlement loss. A bounded-subwindow design must separately account for the persistent credential that produces later window keys; erasing only a leaf is not total erasure.

The ownership-interval counter and commitment give exact protocol attribution of an issued tuple. They do not prove that arbitrary leaked material came from that holder: a holder can rerandomize or export piece keys, and master/previous holders can create other valid keys. This limitation is consistent with optional tracing. The NFT is obligatory in the conforming authorization/delivery path; public NFT state is not itself a confidential key generator or a cryptographic expiry device.

**R11 across deployments.** Public encryption under the same authenticated asset parameters works with every existing key, so successor payloads can use fresh piece randomness without reissuing all credentials. Authority-key rotation need not rotate those parameters. A claimant or publisher can also publish a body with one sidecar per live parameter set, since encryption under any published parameters is public; this is how escrow-era credentials follow later bodies without re-issuance and without the First Finder (S21). A successor under incompatible parameters needs an explicit compatibility/migration mechanism; announcing the same plaintext root does not manufacture one. The registry must reject a purported compatible deployment without the required suite relationship. This remains a protocol rule to specify, not a power given to publishers.

**Public capsule validity.** An authenticated capsule identifies committed bytes, not well-formed components. C11c additionally checks `e(U,F)=e(g1,V)`; C11a checks `e(U,u0)=e(g1,V)` and `e(U,u1)=e(g1,W)`, with group/subgroup validation. Prime order and nondegeneracy force the shared encryption exponent. Every valid native tuple then recovers the same M, preventing malformed capsule components from silently selecting among honest holders. Correct plaintext still needs the authenticated content-integrity rules. These checks add work beyond the table's basic decryption pairings and may be cached for immutable authenticated capsules. A public proof of consistent exponents is an alternative to evaluate, not an omitted cost included in the two-pairing figure.

**Atomic delivery is narrower than perfect fair exchange.** A valid proof and an available envelope can establish that a settled buyer has usable material. They do not hide that material until finality: a buyer may see and decrypt a pending or subsequently reverted transaction. An off-chain buyer check before paying has the same issue. A payment/NFT escrow and proof-bound finalization can prevent ordinary withholding after settlement; strict fairness against premature secret extraction needs its own threat model and construction. No threshold-free secret-release oracle is assumed here.

### Q09 increment — verifiable delivery using linear encryption and a representation proof

**2026-09-21: a concrete algebraic composition, derived here; no implementation or independent security audit.** C11a's two secret group elements can be encrypted to the buyer with linear encryption [E43]. Unlike ordinary ElGamal in a symmetric pairing group, this construction uses the Decision Linear assumption, which is intended to remain hard when DDH is easy. It adds that assumption to BB-WIBE's BDDH assumption. The generalized Schnorr/Fiat-Shamir method [E44] below avoids needing a general arithmetic-circuit proof or a Groth-Sahai setup for this particular relation.

Let the buyer's authenticated encryption public key be `(u,v,h)`, with private scalars `(x,y)` satisfying `u^x=v^y=h`. Choose nonzero x,y and nonidentity group generators; the fixed suite may reuse a public h. The buyer proves possession of x,y and signs this encryption-key binding with its wallet identity. These are separate encryption keys, not a claim that an EVM signing key directly implements linear encryption.

For each native key element `Q` in `(A,B)`, independently choose encryption coins `(r_Q,s_Q)` and publish `E_Q=(E_Q1,E_Q2,E_Q3)=(u^r_Q,v^s_Q,Q*h^(r_Q+s_Q))`. The buyer recovers `Q=E_Q3/(E_Q1^x*E_Q2^y)`. The two elements use independent coin pairs. Thus the complete encrypted native credential is six source-group elements.

Define public values `Y=e(h1,g2)`, `P=e(h,g1)`, `R=e(F_I,h)`, and `T=e(E_A3,g1)/(Y*e(F_I,E_B3))`. Substituting the encrypted values into C11a's validity equation shows that the envelope contains a valid native key exactly when its encryption coins satisfy:

- `E_A1=u^r_A` and `E_A2=v^s_A`;
- `E_B1=u^r_B` and `E_B2=v^s_B`;
- `T=P^(r_A+s_A)*R^(-r_B-s_B)`.

All five are representation equations in prime-order groups, sharing the same four secret scalars. No knowledge of the native key's original randomizer or issuance secret is required. This is a substantial simplification over proving a pairing-based decryption algorithm inside a generic circuit.

**Proof protocol.** Use fresh proof blinders `(b1,b2,b3,b4)` and first messages `V=(u^b1,v^b2,u^b3,v^b4,P^(b1+b2)*R^(-b3-b4))`. Hash a canonical, domain-separated transcript containing the complete public statement, V and exact settlement context to a scalar challenge c. The responses are `z=(b1+c*r_A,b2+c*s_A,b3+c*r_B,b4+c*s_B)` modulo the group order. The verifier checks the four source-group equations, for example `u^z1=V1*E_A1^c`, and the target-group equation `P^(z1+z2)*R^(-z3-z4)=V5*T^c`.

The statement/context includes the chain and contract, asset and immutable parameter digest, exact NFT, current and next ownership/window context, seller and buyer identities, recipient encryption key, envelope digest, transaction purpose and expiry. The wallet signatures and contract predicates authorize that particular state change; the representation proof establishes encrypted-key validity. Hashing context into the proof does not replace the chain's authority and current-state checks.

**Why the algebra gives the required check.** Two accepting transcripts with identical first messages and different challenges recover each encryption scalar by dividing the corresponding response difference by the challenge difference. The first four equations force those scalars to be the envelope's coins. Substitution into the fifth then forces the decrypted `(A,B)` to satisfy the native validity equation. Honest-verifier simulation chooses c,z and reconstructs V, giving the usual Sigma-protocol zero-knowledge argument. Fiat-Shamir's noninteractive knowledge/zero-knowledge claims use the random-oracle model and require a complete composition proof; this paragraph is the algebraic argument, not a production-security certification.

**Costs.** Sending `(c,z1,z2,z3,z4)` lets the verifier reconstruct V before recomputing the hash, so the proof contributes five scalars. The envelope plus proof is therefore `6bG+5bZ`, excluding the recipient public key/proof of possession, wallet signatures, context and transport framing. Here bZ is a scalar's encoding size. This is constant per grant/transfer, independent of payload size, entitlement supply and sale count. Proof verification uses source/target-group exponentiations and the pairings needed for T, P and R; fixed per-asset/per-entitlement pairings can be precomputed. No concrete latency, chain gas cost or proof-library performance is claimed.

The encrypted tuple has a unique plaintext under a well-formed recipient key. Its on-chain digest therefore binds delivery to that tuple without publishing it. A separate publicly checkable hash of the plaintext tuple would require additional proof machinery if exact leaked-key matching is wanted; it is optional tracing functionality. Fresh native keys still rely on correct fresh randomization. Different envelope bytes alone do not prove that native keys differ, so that must not become the key-generation rule. The buyer can privately rerandomize its delivered key once more.

**What this closes and what it does not.** It supplies a concrete, compact publicly checkable valid-key envelope for C11a. The linear encryption scheme is CPA-secure; no CCA theorem is claimed for the combined object. Clients must authenticate the committed envelope and capsule before decryption, and the final protocol must analyze substitution, error leakage, replay, concurrent transactions and proof malleability under its precise oracle model. A valid proof does not guarantee swarm availability. Carry the small envelope in an available settlement record or require an explicit data-availability condition; a hash of missing bytes is insufficient. The proof likewise does not prevent pre-finality buyer extraction or make a vanished First Finder deliver a secret. Those remain distinct obligations.

**Second increment (2026-09-21): settlement ordering, freshness and the residual race.** A settlement that (1) locks the buyer's payment, (2) has the seller post the envelope and proof, and (3) has the contract verify the proof and atomically transfer the NFT and release payment removes ordinary withholding in both directions: the seller cannot be paid without posting a valid envelope, and the buyer cannot obtain the envelope before payment is locked. Because a valid proof makes step 3 deterministic, the remaining pre-finality exposure is a dropped or reorganized inclusion of step 2 after the buyer has read it: the buyer then holds a valid tuple without a settled transfer. That is bounded to one entitlement's access and is S01-class — the seller could equally have leaked its own tuple — and the seller may re-post to complete the sale; an expiry or forfeit rule for the locked payment belongs to the protocol specification. Freshness of the buyer's final key does not depend on the seller (S19). The envelope and proof are small enough to live in the settlement record itself, which supplies data availability without a separate helper object; group encoding sizes are implementation-dependent and unmeasured. Nothing here is a fair-exchange theorem. A time-lock or VDF envelope does not close the residual race and is excluded (X20).

### Q09 third increment — verifier environment, type-3 layout, and a source-group delivery proof

**2026-09-21 (log 28): derived here by one maintainer; algebra checked by hand, not independently re-derived, not implemented, not audited.** This increment supersedes the first increment's envelope and proof for the chosen variant C11a; the first increment's relation remains correct in symmetric groups with a target-group-capable verifier.

**Verifier environment, resolved from the specifications.** cryptography.md rules that proofs verified inside a contract are chain-layer artifacts whose scheme follows the target chain's precompiles, and the MVP expects an EVM launch with the chain unselected and the protocol's own chain behind an adapter later. The portable verifier interface is therefore the one every pairing-capable EVM exposes: source-group addition and scalar multiplication, and a check that a product of pairings equals one (E47). BN254 exposes G1 arithmetic and the pairing check everywhere; BLS12-381 additionally exposes G2 arithmetic where the newer precompiles are deployed. No precompile exposes target-group arithmetic or returns a pairing value. Every on-chain check below is written against that interface; the curve stays a parameter. Both curves are type-3, so the symmetric-pairing setting of E34 and E43 is left behind here.

**Type-3 layout for C11a.** Groups `G1, G2, GT` of prime order `p` with `e: G1 × G2 → GT` and no efficient map between `G1` and `G2`. Public parameters `g1, u0, u1 ∈ G1` and `g2, hpub = g2^alpha ∈ G2`; master issuance material is the scalar `alpha` (equivalently `g1^alpha`), retained by the issuer. `F_I = u0 · u1^I ∈ G1`. Native key `(A, B) = (g1^alpha · F_I^r, g2^r)` with `A ∈ G1`, `B ∈ G2`. Rerandomization `(A · F_I^s, B · g2^s)`. Validity `e(A, g2) = e(g1, hpub) · e(F_I, B)`. Master-secret hiding and cross-identity non-convertibility become co-CDH instances (given `g2^r`, compute `F_I^r` or `u1^r` in `G1`). This placement puts the per-holder key's larger element in `G2` and keeps two of the three capsule elements in `G1`, which minimizes sidecar bytes; the mirrored placement costs one more `G2` element per capsule. The type-3 security statement for the depth-one scheme is the asymmetric BB1 selective-identity result (E46, lead), which replaces E34's symmetric theorem for this layout; that citation is an obligation, not yet inspected.

**KEM-form capsule.** Capsule `(U, V, W) = (g2^t, u0^t, u1^t)` with `U ∈ G2` and `V, W ∈ G1`; the encapsulated value is `K = e(g1, hpub)^t`, computed by the encryptor as `Y^t` with `Y = e(g1, hpub)` precomputed once. Decryption: `K = e(A, U) / e(V · W^I, B)`. The piece key is the domain-separated KDF of `K` and the context; the first increment's random target-group message and its `Z` element are dropped, which is the standard identity-based KEM form and the interface the comparison table already credits to BGW. Capsule well-formedness: `e(V, g2) = e(u0, U)` and `e(W, g2) = e(u1, U)`. Capsule bytes in this form: three source-group elements, one of them in `G2`, so about 128 bytes on BN254 and 192 on BLS12-381 with standard compressed encodings; two pairings and one `G1` scalar multiplication per capsule to decrypt. The KEM's security argument is the usual one under the same decisional assumption as the scheme; recorded as our deduction pending the E46 check.

**Envelope under SXDH.** Because DDH is hard in both source groups of a type-3 curve, Decision Linear encryption (E43) is unnecessary; ElGamal in each group suffices. The buyer's encryption key is `pk1 = g1^x ∈ G1` and `pk2 = g2^y ∈ G2` with **independent** secrets `x, y`, each with a Schnorr proof of possession, the pair signed by the wallet. Sharing one secret across the two groups is a hazard: with `g1^x` and `g2^x` both public, `e(g1^(x·rho), g2) = e(g1^rho, g2^x)` is a DDH test in `G1`, and the `G1` ciphertext loses semantic security. Envelope: `E = (C1, C2, D1, D2) = (g1^rho, A · pk1^rho, g2^sigma, B · pk2^sigma)`, two `G1` and two `G2` elements, about 192 bytes on BN254 and 288 on BLS12-381. The buyer recovers `A = C2 / C1^x` and `B = D2 / D1^y` and checks validity locally with three pairings. The first increment's six-element envelope with four coins becomes four elements with two coins.

**The direct validity proof is not precompile-verifiable, and its obvious repair leaks.** Substituting the envelope into the validity equation gives, with public `P = e(pk1, g2)`, `R = e(F_I, pk2)` and `T = e(C2, g2) / (e(g1, hpub) · e(F_I, D2))`, the relation `C1 = g1^rho`, `D1 = g2^sigma`, `T = P^rho · R^(-sigma)`: the first increment's shape with two scalars. Its Sigma protocol has a target-group first message `P^b1 · R^(-b2)` and a target-group verification equation, both outside the precompile interface. Sending the source-group preimages `pk1^b1` and `F_I^(-b2)` in its place is unsound as a repair: from the response `z1 = b1 + c·rho` the verifier computes `pk1^z1 / pk1^b1 = pk1^(c·rho)`, hence `pk1^rho`, which is the ElGamal mask of `C2`, and `A` is exposed (X23). A Groth–Sahai proof (E42) of the same pairing-product statement is precompile-verifiable and constant-size, with a binding CRS derivable from public coins, but is several times larger than the proof below and verified by dozens of pairings; it is the fallback if the chained proof is ever unavailable, not the choice here. A general SNARK over the pairing relation is the cost the first increment was designed to avoid and is not considered.

**Chained source-group proof.** The seller has a source-group witness for its tuple's validity that the direct proof ignores: the tuple is its decryption of the previous interval's envelope. 
- Let the previous envelope on the entitlement's record be `E_o = (C1_o, C2_o, D1_o, D2_o)`, encrypted to the seller's keys `pk1_s = g1^x_s`, `pk2_s = g2^y_s`, and the new envelope `E_n = (C1_n, C2_n, D1_n, D2_n)` to the buyer's `pk1_b, pk2_b`. The seller's held tuple is `(C2_o / C1_o^x_s, D2_o / D1_o^y_s)`, possibly shifted by its own private offset; the sale applies a further offset; write `s` for the total. 
- The seller proves knowledge of `(x_s, y_s, s, rho, sigma)` satisfying six representation equations, three per source group: `pk1_s = g1^x_s`; `C1_n = g1^rho`; `C2_n · C2_o^(-1) = C1_o^(-x_s) · F_I^s · pk1_b^rho`; `pk2_s = g2^y_s`; `D1_n = g2^sigma`; `D2_n · D2_o^(-1) = D1_o^(-y_s) · g2^s · pk2_b^sigma`. 
- The scalar `s` is shared between a `G1` and a `G2` equation, which a generalized Schnorr argument handles with one response. Special soundness: two accepting transcripts with equal first messages extract every scalar; substitution gives `A_n = C2_n / pk1_b^rho = (C2_o / C1_o^x_s) · F_I^s` and `B_n = D2_n / pk2_b^sigma = (D2_o / D1_o^y_s) · g2^s`, the seller's decrypted tuple shifted by `s`, which is valid whenever the previous interval's tuple was. 
- Honest-verifier zero knowledge: the simulator picks the challenge and responses and reconstructs each first message in its own source group. The base case is the mint proof: the issuer proves knowledge of `(alpha, r, rho, sigma)` satisfying `hpub = g2^alpha`; `C1 = g1^rho`; `C2 = g1^alpha · F_I^r · pk1^rho`; `D1 = g2^sigma`; `D2 = g2^r · pk2^sigma`, with `r` shared across groups; extraction gives a tuple of exactly the issued form under the published `hpub`. 
- The induction runs from mint through every sale, anchored by the ledger binding each interval's encryption keys and envelope; the contract supplies `E_o` and `F_I` from the entitlement's own record, so no other entitlement's envelope can be substituted. 
- Private buyer rerandomization after delivery (S19) is preserved: only the total offset enters the next proof, never its split. Publishing every envelope in the settlement record makes the ownership history literally the delegation chain log 14 anticipated. Relation to X22: that row stands as written — linkage adds nothing to correctness where a target-group-capable verifier exists — and the chained proof is adopted here on the changed premise that the verifier has no target-group arithmetic, which X22's disposition did not consider.

**Sizes and verification work.** With Fiat–Shamir the seller sends the challenge and five responses, six scalars, about 192 bytes on either curve. Where the verifier has `G2` scalar multiplication (BLS12-381 under E47's newer precompiles) it recomputes all six first messages and hashes them: about a dozen source-group scalar multiplications and no pairing. Where it has only `G1` arithmetic (BN254) the seller also sends the three `G2` first messages, about 192 further bytes, and the verifier checks each `G2` equation as a pairing product of `G1`-exponentiated elements against fixed `G2` points, `e(g1^z, ·)` terms only, using non-degeneracy of `e(g1, ·)`: twelve pairings, batchable into one product check with hash-derived weights. Mint proofs are the same size class. Prover work is a handful of exponentiations. No gas or latency figure is claimed; the counts are operations against E47's interface.

### Q09 fifth increment — proof sketches and the KEM assumption stated exactly

**2026-09-21 (log 30): reductions at the level of a careful sketch, each naming its hybrid steps and the exact place a standard theorem is invoked. Same maintainer. These are not a paper's proofs; they are what a second maintainer or reviewer should check line by line.**

**Preliminaries invoked.** (P1) Generalized Schnorr protocols for systems of linear representation equations in prime-order groups are complete, 2-special-sound and special honest-verifier zero-knowledge, with responses unique given statement, first message and challenge. (P2) Fiat–Shamir in the random-oracle model turns such a protocol into a non-interactive argument with knowledge soundness by forking, zero knowledge by oracle programming, and simulation soundness when the statement is hashed (E44, E48). (P3) Every group element the contract accepts — registered keys, envelope elements, first messages — is validated as a point of the prime-order subgroup; where a precompile does not perform the `G2` subgroup check the contract must (E47 to confirm per curve). (P4) The digest used for stored envelopes is collision-resistant.

**Proof of (i), delivery soundness.** Let the adversary control the issuer, every seller and any buyers it likes, and interact with the honest contract over polynomially many settlements. Suppose with non-negligible probability some accepted settlement `n` for entitlement `I` records buyer keys `(pk1_b, pk2_b)` and an envelope `E_n` whose decryption under the unique `(x_b, y_b)` with `pk1_b = g1^x_b`, `pk2_b = g2^y_b` is not a valid tuple for `I`. Take the least such `n`. If `n = 0`, run the (P2) extractor on the accepted mint proof to obtain `(alpha, r, rho, sigma)` satisfying `R_M`; then `C1 = g1^rho` gives `C2 / C1^x_b = C2 / pk1_b^rho = g1^alpha · F_I^r` and likewise `D2 / D1^y_b = g2^r`, a tuple of the issued form under the published `hpub = g2^alpha`, contradicting invalidity. If `n > 0`, settlement `n−1` delivered a valid tuple to the seller keys `(pk1_s, pk2_s)` the contract stored, by minimality. The seller supplied `E_o` matching the stored digest, so `E_o` is the envelope of settlement `n−1` by (P4). Run the extractor on the accepted transfer proof to obtain `(x_s', y_s', s, rho, sigma)` satisfying `R_T`; `pk1_s = g1^x_s'` forces `x_s'` to be the discrete log of `pk1_s`, and likewise `y_s'`, so `C2_o / C1_o^x_s'` and `D2_o / D1_o^y_s'` are exactly the seller's valid tuple `(A_o, B_o)`. Substituting, `C2_n / C1_n^x_b = C2_n / pk1_b^rho = A_o · F_I^s` and `D2_n / D1_n^y_b = B_o · g2^s`, a valid tuple with exponent shifted by `s`, contradicting invalidity. Extraction fails with negligible probability per proof and there are polynomially many proofs, so the union bound gives the claim. Nothing in the argument uses the buyer's proof of possession, and the seller's own keys are never assumed honestly generated.

**Proof of (ii), ledger confidentiality.** Game `G0` is the real transcript of parameters, capsules, registered keys, envelopes and proofs over polynomially many honest mints and sales; the adversary is an observer, and the same hybrids serve an insider who holds some tuples and asks about others. `G1`: every proof is replaced by a simulated proof, programming the oracle at each challenge point; distinguishable from `G0` only by an oracle collision, negligible by (P2). From `G1` on, no witness is used anywhere. `G2.k` for `k = 1 … m` over the `m` envelopes: in envelope `k` replace the `G1` mask `pk1^rho` by a uniformly random `G1` element. A distinguisher between `G2.(k−1)` and `G2.k` solves DDH in `G1` on `(g1, pk1, g1^rho, pk1^rho)`: the reduction embeds the instance as that envelope's key and coin, generates every other envelope and key itself, and simulates every proof. `G3.k` likewise replaces the `G2` mask `pk2^sigma` by DDH in `G2`. In the final game every envelope is a pair of uniformly random elements in each group and every proof is simulated, so the transcript is independent of every tuple and of every offset. The chain relation between consecutive envelopes never appears because each mask is independent. For an insider who knows tuple `k` and asks about tuple `k+1 = tuple k` shifted by `s`, the same hybrid on envelope `k+1` hides `F_I^s` and `g2^s`, so the shift is hidden. Simulation soundness (P2) is what allows a joint game in which the adversary also submits proofs that the honest contract must still reject when false while honest proofs are simulated; it is not needed for the pure observer.

**Proof of (iii), non-holder content confidentiality, and the assumption it needs.** After (ii) the observer's view of the KEM is the parameters and capsules alone. For one capsule that view is `(g1, u0, u1, V, W) = (g1, g1^a, g1^b, g1^(a·t), g1^(b·t))` in `G1` and `(g2, hpub, U) = (g2, g2^alpha, g2^t)` in `G2`, and the question is whether `K = e(g1, g2)^(alpha·t)` is distinguishable from random. Call this **Assumption A1**: for random `a, b, alpha, t`, given those eight elements, `e(g1, g2)^(alpha·t)` is indistinguishable from a random target-group element. Many capsules reduce to one by a hybrid in which the simulator, holding `g1^a`, `g1^b` and `g2^alpha` from the instance, generates every other capsule with its own `t_k`. Two facts about A1. First, it is not the three-exponent DBDH statement as written: the target has two exponents, and the reduction that would embed a DBDH instance needs `g1^t` to build `V` and `W`, but any instance that supplies `g1^t` alongside `g2^alpha` is trivially solvable through `e(g1^t, g2^alpha)`; the mirrored layout has the same shape. Second, A1 holds in the generic bilinear group model: the exponents reachable by pairing one `G1` element with one `G2` element are the products of `{1, a, b, a·t, b·t}` with `{1, alpha, t}`, and `alpha·t` is not among them or in their linear span, so a generic adversary has only negligible advantage. The claim therefore stands under A1, which is what the specification must name unless E46's asymmetric theorem is proved under an assumption that implies it; that is now the exact question for the E46 inspection. Adding an independent second `G2` generator does not restore a third exponent, because the master element absorbs it and the observer's view keeps the same shape.

**Proof of (v), non-malleability and replay.** The challenge is `H` of the full statement, the context and the first messages. Any change to the envelope, the parties, the keys, the counters, the purpose or the expiry changes the hash input, so a verifying proof for the altered statement requires either a second preimage of the challenge or a fresh witness, and the former is negligible in the oracle model. An identical `(E_n, π)` replayed for the same statement is rejected by the contract's counter, which the statement fixes. Two buyers competing for one NFT lock against the same counter; the first valid post advances it and the second post's statement no longer matches, so at most one settles and the other lock refunds at expiry.

**What (i)–(v) leave for a second reader.** The forking-lemma bookkeeping for many extractions; the oracle-collision bound in `G1`; the exact statement of simulation soundness used, against E48; and whether E46 gives A1 or A1 must be stated on its own. The Sigma-protocol facts (P1) are textbook and are not re-proved here.

### Q09 seventh increment — extraction and hybrid bookkeeping

**2026-09-21 (log 32): the two second-reader items the fifth increment left open, written out. Same maintainer; the independent re-derivation of the relations is in progress by a second agent and is not assumed here.**

**Multi-proof extraction for claim (i).** The fifth increment's argument takes the least settlement index at which delivery fails. A reduction cannot identify that index directly, because it cannot decrypt envelopes addressed to adversarial buyers. The standard repair is index guessing. Let `N` bound the number of settlements the adversary causes. The reduction picks `i* ← {0, …, N−1}` uniformly, runs the adversary against the honest contract, and if settlement `i*` is accepted applies the E48 Theorem 3 extractor to its proof, rewinding the adversary once to the oracle query that fixed that proof's challenge. If `i*` is the first failing index, the extracted witness contradicts validity exactly as in the fifth increment: at `i* = 0` it exhibits a tuple of the issued form, and at `i* > 0` it exhibits the seller's decryption of settlement `i*−1`, valid by the choice of `i*`, shifted by the extracted offset. If the guess is wrong the reduction learns nothing, so the success probability is at least `(1/N)` times the Theorem 3 extraction probability, which is a polynomial in the adversary's success minus the extraction error `Q/h`. In the pure adversarial-prover game no simulated proofs exist and plain knowledge soundness would do; Theorem 3 is invoked so that the same bound holds in the joint game where honest parties' proofs are simulated. A single rewinding suffices for one proof; there is no compounding across settlements because only one index is extracted.

**Concrete loss terms for claims (ii), (iii) and (v).** Let `m` be the number of envelopes, `J` the number of capsules the observer sees, `Q` the number of oracle queries, `h` the size of the challenge space, and `p` the group order. Step `G0 → G1` (all proofs simulated): the canonical simulator picks challenge and responses and programs the oracle at the resulting first messages; each simulated transcript is distributed exactly as a real one by perfect special honest-verifier zero knowledge of the linear-relation protocol, and the programming fails only if the adversary already queried that point, which for uniform first messages happens with probability at most `Q/p` per proof, so the statistical distance is at most `m·Q/p`. Steps `G2.k` and `G3.k`: one DDH instance per envelope per group, so the total advantage across them is at most `m·(Adv_DDH,G1 + Adv_DDH,G2)`. Claim (iii)'s capsule hybrid: `J` steps, each one decisional BDH-3b instance, so at most `J·Adv_DBDH-3b`. Claim (v): a proof accepted for a statement other than the one hashed requires a fresh challenge collision, at most `Q/h` per attempt. The observer's total advantage against the joint confidentiality of every tuple, offset and capsule value is therefore at most `m·(Adv_DDH,G1 + Adv_DDH,G2) + J·Adv_DBDH-3b + m·Q/p + Q/h`, all terms negligible for polynomial `m, J, Q`. The reduction's running time is the adversary's plus `O(m + J)` group operations. No tightness beyond these linear factors is claimed, and none is needed at the protocol's scale.

The mint proof is ten pairings unweighted, eight weighted. For a verifier with `G2` arithmetic the scalar multiplications are sixteen, in six multi-scalar multiplications, not "about a dozen". The critical path is corrected accordingly.
2. *Persistent credential (fourth increment).* The stated credential omitted the private post-delivery offset. Resolution adopted: the sale base is the fresh decryption of `E_o`, never the held tuple, so no offset is persisted; the credential is `(x, y, E_o, interval index)`, and the reading tuple is decrypted from `E_o` at start-up and optionally rerandomized in memory. Private rerandomization keeps its S19 role and nothing else depends on it.
3. *Simulated proofs (fifth increment, claim (ii)).* The proofs of possession on registered keys must be among the simulated proofs, because the reduction does not know the discrete log of the embedded key; "masked under an independent key" should read "under independent coins", since keys may repeat across a holder's envelopes and a buyer's key is its seller key at the next sale; in the insider variant the hybrids apply only to envelopes addressed to honest keys; and in the final game `C1` and `D1` stay structured while only the masks are random. The per-proof programming failure probability is at most `(Q_H + Q_P)/p`, counting proof queries as well as hash queries.
4. *Registration hygiene.* The contract must reject identity-element keys (`x = 0` or `y = 0`), which the first increment required and the later ones dropped. Added by this maintainer on the same principle: reject a mint whose `F_I` is the identity element, since then `A = g1^alpha` and the holder would receive the master issuance material outright; hitting it requires `I = −a/b` for the unknown logs of `u0, u1`, so it is not reachable by choice, but the check is one comparison.
5. *Custody consistency (noted, no change).* `R_M` requires the prover to hold the scalar `alpha`, which matches parameter sets as the unit of issuance authority and excludes holder-assisted minting, consistent with C11a and F0.

**Standing after reconciliation.** Every research-level Q09 obligation is closed: relations, proof system, composition claims with bookkeeping, assumption identification, and independent re-derivation. What remains is implementation and measurement, and the specification changes that are separate work.

### First Finder and claim — remaining custody decision for C09-C11

The source documents require pre-claim $0 issuance, bootstrapper erasure after durable escrow, no publisher or consumer right conferred by bootstrap, continuing access through deployment 0, and claimant authority without rights drift. C09/C10's former suggestion to retain the bootstrap master then reissue under a new deployment does not establish those properties. The claimant's optional successor does not replace the obligation to keep the original deployment accessible.

The backward dependency is now concrete: local piece decryption needs a valid native credential; mint/transfer needs an existing compatible credential or issuance secret; a first pre-claim grant has no publisher and no earlier consumer. The First Finder can initialize the secret, but after its required erasure some stated mechanism must preserve confidential authorship for later grants and administrative claim. **User observation, 2026-09-21:** the First Finder already knows the secret and is a known participant; why not retain it until claim? This is a valid candidate, F0, rather than a mathematical error. It is not recorded as approval to discard the availability requirement. A malicious First Finder can retain or leak the initial secret even in the erasure design. Honest erasure instead limits later compromise exposure and removes an ongoing dependency when custody has moved elsewhere.

**Bootstrap decision settled (2026-09-21, P14).** The user requires the initial First Finder object to work with its sole available source. Independent custody before publication is therefore not an available requirement. The preceding question about whether to permit that initial state is resolved. Evaluate growth from one credential source to more without replacing the starting case.

**F0 lifecycle.** The First Finder initializes random per-asset issuance material and an escrow record, retains that material as operational custodian, and issues distinct credentials only against valid escrow-controlled grants. The contract, not the First Finder's key, determines legal issuance, price and authority. Existing consumers need no First Finder to read or voluntarily transfer. Once a claimant proves authority, custody delivery encrypts the existing issuance material to its bound encryption key and proves compatibility with the registered public parameters. For C11a, master validity is the simple equation `e(h2,g1)=e(h1,g2)`; the same encrypted-group-element proof technique can verify the handover. Successful handover changes custody and issuance administration while retaining the same parameters and original deployment, so existing native credentials keep working. After acknowledged handover the conforming First Finder erases its copy. Deliberate retention is not prevented, just as it was not prevented at initialization.

The significant requirement choice is consequently **liveness during escrow and claim**, not the mere existence of a First Finder secret. Claimant identity verification may succeed on-chain while secret handover stalls; those are different events and must not be reported as complete equivalent ownership. A bond or recorded identity can motivate cooperation but does not reconstruct a lost key. F1 adds independent available copies; F2 supplies another route where a compatible holder credential suffices. F0 alone meets the lifecycle only under an explicit assumption that its custodian remains available and follows the escrow policy until handover. Whether that temporary single-party dependency is acceptable is a business/availability decision, not a missing cryptographic technique.

*Superseded in part, 2026-09-21 (S21, log 25):* claim completion does not stall on handover. The claimant adopts its own parameters for all future issuance, keeps deployment 0 live, and publishes later bodies with one sidecar per live parameter set, which needs only public parameters. The remaining single-party dependency is pre-claim grant liveness, accepted at bootstrap by P14 and mitigated under C11c by F2. Handover remains available as a shortcut when the First Finder is present.

Deferring all issuance until claim fails current pre-claim requirements; F0 revises erasure and availability rather than satisfying them silently; reinstating threshold custody fails R07. F0/F1 can be evaluated without accepting their assumptions, but a fully conforming protocol cannot be declared until its custody assumption or another complete path is selected. Loss recovery is optional; pre-claim issuance and administrative continuity are not.

**R12 equivalence under F0 with C11 (user statement 2026-09-21, log 22).** After handover of the issuance material under the same parameters and deployment, the claimant can mint, price and administer exactly as an original publisher; escrow-era credentials keep working; and the only difference is that the claimant did not price the escrow-era keys, which the user names as the sole acceptable difference. ### Q10 — custody state machine under F0 and S21, and the R11 registry rule

**2026-09-21 (log 29): derived from F0, S21, P14 and the Q09 increments; a protocol description, not a specification change.**

**Parameter sets.** A parameter set `P = (g1, u0, u1, g2, hpub)` with its master scalar is the unit of issuance authority. The registry marks parameter sets **live** per asset. `P0` is created at bootstrap by the First Finder; `P1` is created at claim by the claimant; a publisher may later create `P2, …` by rotation. An entitlement is bound for life to the parameter set it was minted under, because its identity element `F_I` and its mint envelope are under that set's bases; transfers never change it.

**States and transitions.**
- **S0 Bootstrap.** The First Finder samples `alpha0`, publishes `P0`, encrypts deployment 0 with a sidecar under `P0`, and creates the escrow record. It is the sole credential source (P14).
- **S1 Escrow-live.** The escrow contract authorizes zero-price grants; the First Finder authors each grant's mint envelope and `π_M` under `alpha0`. Holders read locally and transfer by the chained proof with no custodian involved. If the First Finder is unreachable, no new grants complete; nothing else changes.
- **S2 Claimed.** The claimant proves authority on-chain under the source documents' identity-proof rules, samples `alpha1`, and registers `P1` as live. All future issuance is under `P1`. `P0` entitlements continue to read deployment 0 and to transfer under `P0`'s public bases. The First Finder is not involved (S21).
- **S3 Handover, optional.** If the First Finder is reachable it may deliver `alpha0` to the claimant encrypted to the claimant's registered keys with a Schnorr proof that the encrypted scalar satisfies `hpub0 = g2^alpha0`; the claimant then also holds `P0` authority. After acknowledgement the conforming First Finder erases (P08-class residual if it does not). Handover changes nothing for holders.
- **S4 Later bodies.** Every later deployment of the asset carries one capsule sidecar per live parameter set, so `P0` holders follow new bodies without re-issuance. Retirement: a set may be marked retired only when the registry shows no live entitlement under it; a retired set needs no sidecar on subsequent bodies.
- **Voluntary migration.** A `P0` holder may at any time request a replacement credential under `P1`: the claimant mints a `P1` tuple for the same NFT and the registry supersedes the `P0` credential in one settlement, which is the "replacement mutates a member of the set without growing it" case the user allowed (R09, log 22). Refusal leaves the holder whole, so non-deniability is not required of this step. Migration shrinks the `P0` population toward retirement.
- **Rotation as recovery.** A publisher that loses its master scalar cannot mint under that set; it registers a new set and continues, at the cost of one more sidecar per body while the old set has live entitlements. Custody of the master scalar is the publisher's liability (P13 applied to the publisher).

**Invariants.** No transition ever requires action by an existing holder or affects its ability to read or transfer. Deployment 0 stays live. Every live set has a sidecar on every body. Legal issuance authority is always the registry's, never a key's.

**R11 registry rule.** A deployment record for an asset is accepted only if it lists a capsule sidecar for every parameter set the registry marks live for that asset at the time of the record; a record that omits one is rejected as incompatible, and "same plaintext root" claims do not substitute. This closes the R11 migration item: there is no incompatible-parameter case, because compatibility is defined as coverage of live sets.

**Growth beyond one source.** Not required. If replication is wanted with C11a, F1 with the depth-two variant (F1 row, log 28) is the recorded route; sharing `alpha` itself among custodians is F1 as originally described.

### Q05 — window, storage and attempt semantics: conclusions

**2026-09-21 (log 29).** These settle interpretations 1 and 5 at the research level; adopting them into cryptography.md and MVP Scope.md is separate work (discipline item 8).

**Window.** The cryptographic window is the ownership interval. Each interval has exactly one native tuple and one envelope key pair; no subwindow keys exist. The MVP's bounded subwindows existed to force re-provisioning through the provisioner; with no provisioner and per-attempt state checks, a subwindow adds nothing for a conforming client, and against a modified client it bounds nothing, since retained material cannot be expired (S01). Any volume or time limit within an interval is conforming-client policy outside the cryptographic scope. Interpretation 3's retained-root question is answered by naming the persistent credential exactly: `(tuple, x, y, E_o, interval index)`, constant size, destroyed at interval end under the rule below; there is no root that regenerates later window keys because there are no later window keys.

**Attempt.** An attempt is one derivation of a piece key from one capsule, hence one per capsule or piece group. Before an attempt the conforming client requires: a state view at the chain adapter's SOFT tier, no older than `τ_soft`, showing the wallet as owner of the exact NFT at the credential's interval index; a wallet-control assertion for the session, renewed every `τ_wallet`; and the authenticated payload descriptor for the deployment. `τ_soft` and `τ_wallet` are suite parameters chosen at implementation against the adapter's tiers, not research quantities.

**Transitions and reorganizations.** On observing a transfer out at SOFT, the client stops new attempts; it destroys the credential and all derived material only when that transfer reaches the HARD tier, so a reorganized transfer never strands a still-owner. On a transfer in, the client uses the new credential once the settlement is at SOFT and the decrypted tuple passes the local validity check. A cached state proof older than `τ_soft` is not proof of current ownership.

**Offline.** No attempts without a fresh state view. The user's stated model is that the bearer client is online for the swarm in any case (log 22 text under interpretation 7), so this is the existing posture, not a new restriction; latency is that of one state read per `τ_soft`, accepted under P11.

**Helper budget (interpretation 4), by formula.** Per capsule: three source-group elements, one of them in `G2`. Per interval on chain: a four-element envelope plus a six-scalar proof, plus three `G2` elements on BN254. Per holder persistent: the credential above, constant. Accumulation: linear in intervals in chain history, constant per holder, none per read. For a 16 KiB piece group the sidecar is below one percent of payload on either curve, and for a 1 MiB group a few hundredths of a percent. Byte measurement on a chosen curve remains the only open part.

### Q11 — chain walk against a quantum adversary

**2026-11-11 (log 38): derived here by one maintainer from the C11 section and the Q09 third and fourth increments; not independently re-derived. The adversary is assumed to compute discrete logarithms in `G1`, `G2` and the signature curves. No cost model for that computation is used or claimed.**

**Published objects and what each determines.** "Retroactive" means from public, permanent material alone, whenever the adversary arrives; "at use" means only by acting at that time.

**The target.** Content confidentiality falls from the sidecar and the parameter set alone: one discrete logarithm, of `hpub`, opens every capsule of every deployment under that set, with no ledger history, no credential and no holder involved. The ledger's envelopes are a second, independent path to the same end. Every deployment ever seeded stays exposed for as long as its sidecar exists anywhere, and a successor deployment protects only what is encrypted after it. Nothing in the present construction contributes to the target; it is wholly Q12 and Q14.

**The minimum splits, and most of it survives.** Three properties sit under "soundness at issuance and use".

- *Delivery validity survives.* `R_M` and `R_T` are false for an invalid envelope: the first-group equations fix every scalar, the shared scalar among them, and the last second-group equation is then a constraint with no free variable. A false statement has no witness, so the Sigma protocols' special soundness is statistical and holds against an unbounded prover; a settlement the contract accepts still delivered a valid credential for the exact entitlement to the registered keys. What a quantum adversary gains is the honest witness of a true statement, which is a loss of confidentiality, not of soundness. Under Fiat–Shamir this needs the transform to preserve soundness against superposition queries (E53, abstract only); the generic search for a favourable challenge over a field of about `2^255` is on the order of `2^127` quantum queries, our arithmetic.
- *Authority does not survive where the signer's scheme is a discrete-logarithm scheme.* A transfer needs the seller's envelope secrets and a signed intent from a signer the seller's account admits; the adversary computes the first and, under secp256k1, the second, and the settlement it authors is one the contract is right to accept. The same holds for a mint under the issuance authority's signer. Where the signer is a post-quantum key, the adversary holds the witness and cannot use it: the account's signer set is keyed by scheme-tagged key identifiers and verified through the adapter bound per scheme, so authority is restored by the signature layer alone, at the cost of verifying that scheme on chain (Q15).
- *Exclusive issuance does not survive as a cryptographic property.* With `alpha` anyone authors a credential for any identity. An entitlement is still a ledger act under the issuance authority's intent, so this is a loss of the target, reading without an entitlement, and not a second loss of the minimum.

So the minimum reduces to signatures and to the chain's own integrity. It does not need a different proof system for the present relations, and a different proof system alone would not help the target (S24).

**Outside the construction's control.** The submitting transaction's own signature, which carries no authority under the signed-intent rule; Base's sequencer and Ethereum's consensus signatures; the EVM's twenty-byte account form; the source's signing keys; the platform artifact signatures. The minimum is conditional on the chain's history staying intact. While it does, a signature or proof settled before a quantum adversary exists keeps its meaning by ledger order.

**Margins against category 3.** Categories as NIST defines them (E50): key search on AES-128, AES-192 and AES-256 are categories 1, 3 and 5; collision search on a 256-bit and a 384-bit hash are categories 2 and 4.

Collision resistance is what a binding rests on wherever one party chooses both inputs: an asset's root against its registrant, an envelope digest against the seller that later presents the envelope, a parameter-set digest against its issuer, the identity mapping against whoever can choose entitlement identifiers, a key commitment against its registrant. Each sits one category below the target, and a quantum adversary does not move it. Reaching category 3 there means a wider commitment scheme and digests that are not single scalars, which the hash-card's commitment-scheme identifier and length-carrying fields admit as further concretes.

**The nine steps.** Step 1 and step 2 hold. Step 3 fails retroactively: the encapsulated value is public to this adversary. Step 4 holds in kind at the hash margins above. Step 5 fails retroactively. Step 6 holds for delivery validity and fails at use for authority under a discrete-logarithm signer. Step 7 holds as structure. Step 8 is unchanged: the per-attempt rule binds only a conforming client, as it always did (S01), and what the adversary gains is entitlement theft through step 6, not a bypass of the attempt rule. Step 9's assumptions all fall for confidentiality; the symmetric and hash components hold in kind.

**Not done.** The BLAKE3 paper's own security claims, whose text could not be extracted; the full text of E53 and its conditions; an independent re-derivation of the delivery-validity argument; any cost model for the discrete-logarithm computation.

**Correction to the margins (log 39), from the BLAKE3 paper's text (E52).** The designers target 128-bit security for every goal, second preimage and keyed use included, so the margin table's second-preimage row is withdrawn and its keyed-derivation row is settled: every BLAKE3 use in the construction, the content roots under either attack, the wrapping-key derivation, the publisher lineage and the keyed plaintext-root mode, is claimed at a 128-bit level, the class of category 1, two below the target for key search and one below for collision. This is a claimed design level, not an attack, and a quantum adversary does not change it. Reaching category 3 on these components means a further hashing concrete and a further KDF concrete under their own identifiers, with a commitment scheme whose internal state is wider than 256 bits (S26). The paper's rule for derive_key, a hardcoded context string with variable data in the key material, is recorded on E52 as a property any BLAKE3 derivation concrete is to be read against.

**Delivery validity under the transform, closed (log 41), from the supplied text of E53.** Corollary 13: for a Sigma protocol with a superpolynomial challenge space that is statistically sound against a static adversary, its Fiat–Shamir transform is statistically sound against an adaptive adversary with quantum access to the oracle; Theorem 8 gives the loss as a factor of order `q^2` in the number of oracle queries. The protocols for `R_M` and `R_T` are statistically sound, since for a false statement and a fixed first message at most one challenge has an accepting response, and their challenge space is the scalar field. S24's reliance on E53 is therefore discharged at the level of the stated corollary, and the `2^127` query figure stands as the generic bound. The paper's warning that special soundness does not give a proof of knowledge against a quantum prover does not touch this: delivery validity needs soundness, not extraction. What remains under Q11 is the independent re-derivation alone, which the user has deferred to one pass over the whole question once every undetermined item is first resolved (log 41).

### C12 — Lattice preimage credentials with coset rerandomization (Q12)

**Status, 2026-11-12 (log 40): a candidate path derived here by one maintainer from the general shape of the lattice identity-based constructions. Every source behind it was read at abstract level only (E55–E60). No algorithm was checked against a paper's text, no proof exists, no parameter was chosen. It is a lead with a stated list of what would make it a construction, not a construction.**

**The base scheme, as generally described and not yet checked against E56.** Public matrix `A` over `Z_q` with `n` rows and `m` columns; the master secret is a short basis of the kernel lattice `{z : A·z = 0 mod q}`. An identity maps to a target `u_I`. A credential is a short vector `e` with `A·e = u_I mod q`. A capsule is an LWE sample pair `(c0, c1) = (A^T·s + x, u_I^T·s + x' + encode(mu))`, and decapsulation computes `c1 − e^T·c0`, which is `encode(mu)` plus a small error for any short preimage `e`.

**What carries over from C11 directly.** Our deductions from that shape:

- *Payload compatibility.* Every short preimage of `u_I` recovers the same `mu` from the same capsule, so one sidecar entry serves every holder (R01), and the capsule's cost does not depend on how many credentials exist (R10).
- *Public credential validity.* `A·e = u_I` and a norm bound are checkable from public parameters alone.
- *Issuance at mint under the master secret, nobody on the read path* (S17's family, R07, R08).
- *Asset scope.* One fixed target for the parameter set, as C11c fixes one identity.

**Authorship at sale without the master secret is the hard property, and three routes exist.**

- *Route 1, none.* The seller hands over its preimage. This is C09's bearer transfer: it reaches reading (a) of interpretation 7 and not (a′).
- *Route 2, the credential is a basis.* A holder that holds a short basis of its identity's lattice can sample fresh preimages and fresh bases itself. The sampler's width is essentially the longest Gram–Schmidt length of the basis it samples with (E56's abstract), so what a holder can author is no better than what it holds and in general worse: each delegation costs quality, the parameters must be fixed for a maximum chain length, and a basis is far larger than a preimage. This is C10's shape with a transfer-depth bound, which the credential interface forbids. Whether E59 states the growth exactly is unchecked.
- *Route 3, coset rerandomization over published kernel hints.* Publish with the parameter set a short basis `Z` of a rank-`k` sublattice of the kernel lattice. A holder of `e` samples a fresh `e'` from a discrete Gaussian over the coset `e + L(Z)` at a fixed width set by `Z` alone. `e'` is again a short preimage of the same target, the distribution depends on the coset and not on which representative the holder started from, so nothing grows across any number of sales, the credential stays one short vector, and no master secret is used. This is C11's shape: C11 rerandomizes along the public direction `(F_I, g2)`, and here the public directions are `Z`.

**What Route 3 needs to be true.** Confidentiality against non-holders must survive the publication of `k` short kernel vectors, and a holder of `e_I` together with `Z` must be unable to produce a short preimage of another target `u_J`. Both are statements about lattice problems with kernel hints. E55 introduces k-LWE as exactly the LWE variant in which short kernel vectors are given, and reduces LWE to it with polynomial loss in `k` (abstract only); its range of `k`, its noise conditions, and whether its hints may be published rather than held by users were not read. The non-convertibility statement, an inhomogeneous preimage problem with hints under identity-key queries, was found in no source and would be ours to prove.

**Where Route 3 is weaker than C11, by our deduction.** A rerandomized credential is fresh only within its coset of a rank-`k` sublattice, so its entropy is bounded by `k` and the width, and two credentials of one identity are publicly linkable by their difference lying in `L(Z)`; C11's tuples of one identity are likewise linkable through the pairing. Every credential of one identity lies in one coset of a published lattice, so unlimited sales reveal no kernel vector outside `L(Z)`, and the issuer releases one preimage per target, which is the base scheme's own rule.

**Second increment (2026-11-12, log 41): the supplied texts of E55, E56, E57, E59 and E60, read at the passages cited on their evidence rows, not in full.**

- *The base scheme is confirmed.* E56 builds the identity-based scheme as a dual of Regev's encryption under LWE in the random-oracle model, with individual keys of order `n^2` bits, and ties the sampler's width to the quality of the basis. E57's Theorem 5.1 proves that same scheme secure when the oracle is quantum-accessible.
- *Route 2 is excluded on the text (X24).* E59's RandBasis takes a basis `S` and a parameter `s` at least the basis's Gram–Schmidt norm times `ω(√log n)`, and returns a basis of norm up to `s·√m` whose distribution is the same whichever basis of the lattice it started from (Lemma 3.3). A holder therefore cannot author a basis as good as its own: every sale multiplies the bound by at least `√m·ω(√log n)`, and the parameters must be fixed for a maximum number of sales.
- *E55's scheme is the asset-scope credential KEM, already built.* Each user holds a distinct short vector in a shared lattice, one common ciphertext serves them all, and the scheme stays secure while a coalition holds fewer than `m/c` keys for a constant `c`. Read as a credential KEM this is master-issued distinct credentials under one fixed target, with unbounded issuance. Under the asset scope a coalition recovering the lattice's short basis is not a loss, since holder authorship is that scope's purpose.
- *E55's problem is not Route 3's premise as stated.* In Definition 15 the `k` hints are themselves short preimages of the target, so each one decrypts, and the challenge is uniform only over the orthogonal complement of their span. Route 3 publishes differences of preimages, which are kernel vectors of the matrix alone. For such hints the complement leaves the message-bearing coordinate unconstrained, so indistinguishability over that complement would hide the message from a party holding only the differences; this is our observation. It needs the hardness result for a hint distribution the paper does not treat: the paper says Theorem 18 adapts readily to hints drawn over the whole kernel of the augmented matrix, which is a different distribution again. This is the one named lemma Route 3's confidentiality rests on.
- *Parameters.* Theorem 18's example is `k = m/10`, `σ = Θ(n)`, `σ′ = Θ(n^5)`, `q = Θ(n^5)`, `m = Θ(n log n)`, over unstructured lattices. That is polynomial and far from E58's 2 to 4 kilobytes; nothing read extends the result to structured lattices.
- *Two leads from E55's own open problems.* The authors call their scheme a good starting point for an identity-based traitor-tracing scheme, which is the entitlement scope's shape. They also record as open a chosen-ciphertext-secure lattice scheme in which different independent keys serve one public key, because the known techniques check a ciphertext's well-formedness with the full short basis; that bears directly on the missing capsule check, and the re-encryption check proposed above is not established by anything read.
- *Delivery, from E60's Table 1.* A verifiably encrypted short solution costs a 9 KB proof and a 9 KB ciphertext at the smallest sample set, whose plaintext has entries of size one, and 54 KB and 71 KB at the set whose plaintext entries reach `2^18`. A credential is a short vector with entries well above one, so an envelope and its proof would run from tens to over a hundred kilobytes per settlement, against about half a kilobyte now. Extraction is relaxed: decryption recovers a witness of an extended language, so the delivered credential is guaranteed short only up to the relaxation, and the norm bound must carry that slack.

**Third increment (2026-11-12, log 42): the two arguments Route 3 lacked, attempted. Derived here by one maintainer; sketches, not proofs; no source states either.**

**The kernel-hint lemma, asset scope.** Setting: matrix `A`, one target `u`, a capsule `(A^T·s + x, u^T·s + x' + encode(mu))`. The issuer draws short preimages `x_0, …, x_k` of the target as E55's Definition 15 draws its hints, publishes the differences `z_i = x_i − x_0`, which are short kernel vectors of `A`, and keeps or erases `x_0`. Claim: a party holding `A`, `u` and the `z_i` and no preimage cannot distinguish the capsule's message.

- *Step 1.* By the hardness of E55's problem with `k + 1` hints, the capsule is indistinguishable from a noisy uniform point of the orthogonal complement of the span of all `k + 1` augmented hints, even to a party holding the hints themselves. The reduction holds `x_0, …, x_k`, computes the differences and hands over less than it has.
- *Step 2.* That complement is the complement of the differences' span cut by one further equation, the one `x_0` imposes: it fixes the message-bearing coordinate as an inner product of `x_0` with the rest. To a party without `x_0` that inner product is one evaluation of a universal hash keyed by the point, on an input `x_0` it does not know; if `x_0` keeps enough min-entropy given `A`, `u` and the differences, modulo the differences' span, the leftover hash lemma makes the coordinate statistically uniform.
- *Step 3.* With that coordinate uniform and independent, the message is hidden.

The entropy condition is the lemma's price. Conditioned on the differences, `x_0` is a Gaussian narrowed by a factor of about `√(k+1)`, and the quotient by the span costs up to `k·log q` bits, so the condition reads roughly `m·log(σ/√(k+1)) ≥ (n + k + 1)·log q` plus a security margin, with the narrowed width still above the kernel lattice's smoothing parameter. On E55's example parameters at `k = m/10` this is borderline by our arithmetic, so `k` is a smaller constant fraction of `m` or the width is larger. Unchecked: the exact conditional distribution, the form of the complement over the torus E55 works in, and that the differences' law does not depend on which target the preimages were drawn for.

With the lemma, the asset scope is argued end to end at sketch level: E55's scheme gives master issuance, the published differences give authorship at sale at constant size, a non-holder learns nothing, and non-convertibility is not a property that scope has.

**The entitlement scope does not carry over, for a structural reason (S27).** A common capsule that every entitlement opens under its own identity is the all-wildcard pattern of C11a. Its lattice form, in the shape of the standard identity-matrix constructions, is a credential `(e1, e2)` with `A·e1 + (B_0 + I·B_1)·e2 = u` for a small identity `I`, and a capsule carrying LWE samples under `A`, `B_0` and `B_1` separately so that the holder combines the last two with its own `I`. Then `(e1, e2, I·e2)` is a short preimage of the one target under the one wide matrix `[A | B_0 | B_1]`, for every entitlement. Three consequences, our deductions:

- Any short preimage under the wide matrix opens the capsule whether or not it has the form of a credential, so material that opens capsules need not attribute to any entitlement.
- Two credentials of different entitlements differ by a short kernel vector of the wide matrix. A coalition of about as many holders as the wide lattice has dimensions spans a full-rank short set and can then produce such unattributable material at will. Master hiding and attribution hold only below that coalition size. C11a holds them against every coalition, because there the linear relations among credentials sit in exponents nobody can read; here they sit in the clear and only shortness guards them.
- Rerandomization hints for an entitlement are short kernel vectors of the same wide matrix. Published, they accumulate across entitlements and bound the total number of entitlements that may ever be minted, against R10. Held privately, they accumulate across a coalition and lower the bound above.

The coalition bound is E55's `m/c` appearing for the reason E55's own open-problem paragraph gives: each user owns part of one shared secret. Over the compact structured lattices that give kilobyte sizes the bound is much worse, since each credential yields as many independent vectors as the ring has dimensions and the coalition needed falls to about the module's rank. Compactness and coalition resistance trade against each other directly in this family.

**State after the third increment.** Under the asset scope, a post-quantum credential KEM with authorship at sale exists at sketch level, on E55's hardness result and one entropy condition. Under the entitlement scope, non-convertibility and master hiding against every coalition are not available in this family as derived; a bounded-coalition form is, with the bound set by the lattice dimension and collapsing under structured lattices. Non-convertibility was recorded as a stronger, non-mandatory property (S15), and it is the first place where P16 under unchanged constraints meets a limit: what would bend is the entitlement scope's attribution, either to a published coalition bound or to the asset scope for every suite. That choice is Q16's and the user's, not made here. Both derivations are written out in [cryptography-pq-credential-kem-derivation.md](cryptography-pq-credential-kem-derivation.md) (log 43).

### Q14 and Q15 — envelope, delivery proof and verifier environment for C12's asset-scope form

**2026-11-12 (log 43): one maintainer, from E60 at the passages on its evidence row, E61, and the gas reports gathered at log 38's survey of on-chain signature verification; nothing measured, nothing implemented. The entitlement-scope choice held for Q16 does not bear on either question: both are worked for the asset-scope form.**

**Q14. The statement becomes linear, and the chain of envelopes drops out.** C11's validity equation is a pairing equation, which is why Q09 proved delivery by chaining each envelope to the previous one. A lattice credential's validity is `A·e = u` with a norm bound, a linear relation with a short witness, which is exactly the statement class E60's verifiable encryption proves about its plaintext. So mint and transfer use one proof: the envelope encrypts a short preimage of the target to the buyer's registered key. Three consequences, our deductions. The seller's witness is its credential and its coins, not its envelope secrets, so losing the envelope secrets no longer ends the ability to sell. The contract needs no previous envelope as a statement, so nothing is recovered from chain history to make a sale. And the buyer's key is an ordinary lattice encryption key, registered as today.

**Q14, what the relaxation costs.** E60's soundness guarantees that decryption returns a witness of an extended language. For this statement that is a short vector mapping to a small multiple of the target, not to the target itself. Such a vector opens a capsule only if the capsule's encoding and noise budget absorb the multiple. So delivery validity in this form reads: an accepted settlement delivered material that opens the asset's capsules, provided the KEM's parameters are chosen with that slack; an honest seller's envelope decrypts exactly. Fixing that slack is a parameter obligation this increment does not discharge.

**Q14, confidentiality and the oracle model.** E60 is secure under Ring-LWE in the random-oracle model with a simulation-sound relaxed proof, the same claim structure as Q09's envelope claims. P16's minimum needs soundness with a quantum-accessible oracle; E53 treats a lattice Sigma protocol of the aborting kind under a stated assumption (its Assumption 27 and Corollary 28), located and not read, and whether that covers E60's protocol is open.

**Q15, verification must stay in the contract, and the data must stay available (X25).** Carrying the envelope and proof as swarm objects with only their hash on chain was proposed by the user (log 43). It would cut the fee, and it removes two things the settlement rests on. The contract releases payment because it verified the proof in the same transaction; with only a hash it has verified nothing, and a seller is paid for a hash of anything. And a hash of withheld bytes delivers nothing, which the Q09 first increment already ruled on: carry the envelope in an available settlement record or require an explicit availability condition. The repairs each cost something else: a buyer's acknowledgement before payment lets the buyer take the credential and withhold the acknowledgement; a challenge window delays settlement and still cannot prove that bytes were withheld; a succinct proof of correct verification must itself be sound against a quantum adversary, which excludes the pairing-based ones. Blob space does not help directly: blob contents are not readable by contract execution, only a commitment is, and the opening check is a pairing-based commitment scheme (E61).

**Q15, where the cost actually lands.** On an L2 the transaction's bytes are already posted to L1 by the rollup itself, so a settlement's size is paid as the L1 data fee plus the L2's own charge for calldata, and the verification as L2 execution. For an envelope and proof of 18 to 125 KB that is a large multiple of the present half kilobyte, and of the same order as a few seconds of the content the user's log 42 observation compares it to. Verification is polynomial arithmetic and hashing with no precompile; the nearest measured analogue is on-chain verification of the lattice signature, reported at 1.23M gas for the smallest standard parameter set in an optimized verifier and far more in an unreviewed attempt at the middle set. No figure for E60's verifier exists in anything read. The free path is where this binds: every sponsored mint and grant would carry it.

**Q15, the minimum's signer.** S24 reduced the minimum to a post-quantum signer on the identity account. The measured point is the smallest lattice parameter set, category 2, at 1.23M gas; the middle set, P16's target, has only draft precompile proposals and an unreviewed measurement. A lead not yet sourced: one-time hash-based signatures verify with hash calls alone, which contract execution prices cheaply, and the account already keeps a nonce, so the statefulness such schemes need is already there. Their level against the category target follows S25 and S26's reasoning for the hash used and is unchecked.

**State.** Q14 is answered in shape: an envelope and one proof for both mint and transfer, from E60, with the relaxation carried by parameters and the quantum-oracle soundness open. Q15 is answered in shape: verification and data stay on chain; the cost is real, unmeasured, and falls on the free path; the minimum's signer is feasible today one category below the target. Neither is measured.

### Q16 — boundary report: how far P16 reaches under unchanged constraints

**2026-11-12 (log 44): one maintainer, assembling Q11 to Q15. Every conclusion beneath it is at sketch level, unmeasured and not independently re-derived; this report inherits all of that.**

**What drives settlement cost, answering the user's question.** Three terms, in the order they matter on the launch network. First, bytes: on a rollup every byte of the transaction is posted to the L1, and the network's own documentation says that fee is typically the larger one (E63); it scales linearly with the envelope and proof, and it moves with the L1's data market. Second, compute: with no precompile for lattice arithmetic, every modular multiplication of a verifier is ordinary contract execution, and a hash other than the natively priced one is far dearer still; for a signature of a few kilobytes this term dominates, and for an envelope of a hundred kilobytes the bytes do. The bytes are charged a second time as execution gas, 16 per non-zero byte with a floor of 40 for data-heavy transactions (E64). Third, storage, which is a digest and a counter and is small. On the dollar figure: the network documents about $0.002 for 200,000 gas at its minimum base fee and an ETH price of $2,000 (E63), which puts ten million gas of execution near ten cents there; a figure of tens of dollars per settlement is what the same gas costs on the L1 itself at a few gwei, our arithmetic. The L1 data fee for a hundred kilobytes on the launch network was not found in any source and is the unmeasured term. So the cost is a large multiple of today's and unacceptable on the free path at L1 prices, and its size on the launch network is unknown, not known to be prohibitive.
